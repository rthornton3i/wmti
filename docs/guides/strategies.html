Strategies

In short: a strategy is a choice that changes how much tax you pay over the years, such as when to claim Social Security or whether to convert Traditional money to Roth. To judge one, save a scenario with it and one without, and compare the two; or let Optimize search for you.

This page is for anyone weighing a strategy in the app. It lists the choices you can compare in the toolkit today, what each one changes, and where to compare it; the home-state treatment and the rules the model leaves out are in Tax law in the model. Save one config per choice and open Compare (see Using the toolkit): both configs run on the same random draws, so the difference comes from the setting. Everything below is a model of the law in the law files (tax-year-update), not tax advice.

Strategies you can compare today

Choice

What it changes

Where to set it

Withdrawal order

Priority drains accounts in the order you rank them (for example cash, then Investments, then Traditional, then Roth); proportional draws from every account by balance. Required distributions are taken first either way, so a late Traditional balance cannot be avoided.

Accounts tab: strategy and priority; or Optimize (Finding the best settings)

Traditional or Roth saving

The share of pay deferred into a Traditional 401k (lowers taxable pay now) against a Roth deposit (taxed now, untaxed later). The employer match goes into the Traditional balance. The Roth IRA part of a deposit shrinks across the income phase-out; a Roth 401k has no income limit.

Benefits tab (Retirement plan)

Where surplus cash goes

How unspent cash (the surplus) is split over the cash, Investments and 529 accounts. Investments sales are taxed only on the gain share, so holding more cash or more Investments changes the tax on later withdrawals.

Accounts tab: surplus sliders; or Optimize

When to claim Social Security

The benefit and the years it starts. Taxable benefits count withdrawals and gains, so the claim age can change the tax on the same withdrawals.

Retirement tab: benefit table (entered and estimated ages 62 to 70) and claim age; Compare tab: two saved configs with different claim ages; or Optimize

Health premium before or after tax

A premium paid before tax lowers taxable pay, up to the pay left after the Traditional deposits and the dependent care FSA (with no pay it is paid after tax); one paid after tax can be itemized above the medical floor. Medicare premiums are always after tax.

Benefits tab (Medical plan); Medicare on the Retirement tab

Using an HSA

A pre-tax HSA payment of the year’s healthcare cost lowers taxable income; withdrawals are untaxed.

Benefits tab (HSA) and Accounts tab

Itemizing or the standard deduction

Chosen automatically each year, whichever is larger. Mortgage interest, state and property tax (with the SALT cap), medical costs and charity can tip it; separate returns must both itemize if either does. From 2026 a return on the standard deduction also deducts cash gifts to charity up to 1,000 (2,000 joint), so itemizing wins only above the standard deduction plus that amount. From 2025 to 2028 each person aged 65 or older adds a senior deduction of 6,000, itemizing or not; each person’s 6,000 falls by 6% of income above 75,000 (150,000 joint), so a couple both 65 or older has none left at 250,000; separate returns get none. Neither reaches the state tax. Charity rows are also charged each year as cash leaving the household (Charitable Giving).

Expenses tab (rows with a tax deduction, Charity rows, property taxes), Loans tab (Mortgage loans), Benefits tab (premiums), Healthcare tab (visits, drugs, Medicare)

Filing jointly or separately

Joint returns use the joint brackets and limits; separate returns use the married-filing-separately brackets, a half SALT cap and a zero Social Security threshold. Each account’s income goes on its owner’s return; the split of JOINT account income and shared items between the two returns is set on Setup.

Setup: filing status

Home state

The state brackets, exclusions and add-backs of any of the 50 states or DC.

Setup: home state

Paying down a loan

Extra payments, a refinance or forgiveness change the payoff date, the interest paid and the mortgage interest you can itemize.

Loans tab

How the cost of care is modelled

Doctor visits and drugs every year, counted while healthcare costs are included.

Healthcare tab

The Strategies tab holds three strategies, each off by default so a saved config that does not use them is unchanged: the Roth conversion, the Roth backdoor and the QCD. The tab also holds the Relocation section after them (a move to another state, off by default; see Relocation: moving to another state), the starting after-tax basis of the Traditional IRAs (Form 8606, in the Roth conversion pane), a note on how the home state treats them (How the home state treats the strategies), and warnings for what the model leaves out for the settings chosen. The beneficiaries and the terminal tax rate are not a strategy: they are the Beneficiaries section of the Benefits tab (see Terminal tax rate). The Summary tab shows the beneficiaries’ tax used to judge the strategies after tax, with a link to that section.

Roth conversions

Roth conversions are set on the Strategies tab: an age window (your age, both ends included), an optional amount per year in today’s value, and ceilings that all apply (a federal bracket top, an IRMAA tier, a Social Security limit, the 0% gains bracket top or a custom AGI), the lowest one winning. With no amount, the model converts as much as the ceilings allow; with an amount and no ceiling, it converts the amount. Preset buttons switch the conversion on under one ceiling alone.

Each year in the window, the required distributions and the year’s withdrawals come first and are never converted. The model then finds the largest amount that keeps every ceiling on every return (separate returns each carry their share) with a bracketed root finder: each trial amount reruns the year’s whole withdrawal and tax computation, so the conversion’s own tax is paid by further withdrawals and effects such as the Social Security “torpedo” or a switch to itemizing are exact. A conversion is ordinary income but not cash: it moves money from the Traditional accounts to a Roth account of the same owner (IRA money to the owner’s first Roth IRA, 401k money to the owner’s first Roth 401k, else the owner’s first Roth IRA), shows as Roth Conversion with its taxable part in Conversion Taxable, and never pays a bill. The window is your age even for a partner-owned account (the tab warns).

The rollover account. Switching the conversion on when someone who owns a Traditional account has no Roth account of their own to receive it (no Roth IRA or Roth 401k, or a Traditional IRA and no Roth IRA) adds a Roth IRA named Rollover account for that owner (Rollover account (partner) for the partner): worth 0, with the usual Roth IRA returns, no surplus share and the last withdrawal priority. Switching the conversion off removes it again while it is untouched; editing it in any field (name, kind, owner, starting value, surplus share, returns, basis or deposit) makes it your own account, which is never removed (isUntouchedRollover is the one rule for the switch and the optimizer). The account carries autoCreated in the saved config (schema version 27, which marks every account saved before as not added), and a config loaded with the conversion on and no Roth account to convert into gets its rollover account at load, so Traditional money is never stranded for want of a Roth account. Configs whose owners already have a Roth account are unchanged.

Early-retiree conversion ladder. The years between the last paycheck and Social Security or the first RMD often have the lowest taxable income of a lifetime. Set the window to those years and a ceiling such as the top of the 12% or 22% bracket to fill the low brackets each year; the Traditional balance, and the RMDs and taxable Social Security it drives later, fall. Before age 59.5 the model has no 10% penalty and no Roth five-year rule, so the ladder’s waiting period is not modelled (the tab warns). Conversions before the Medicare age raise the income an ACA premium credit is tested on, which the model does not price (the tab warns too). The enhanced credits expired after 2025, so from 2026 the credit ends completely above 400% of the federal poverty level (about 62,600 for one person, 84,600 for two; the warning counts you, your partner and the children still in their child years at the first conversion); married couples filing separately get none.

Medicare IRMAA and the two-year lookback

The IRMAA surcharge applies whenever a Medicare premium is charged: Part B on, or a Part D premium above zero, on the Retirement tab (Medicare). The surcharge is charged on each return’s AGI from two years earlier (the law file’s lookback_years), against that later year’s thresholds (the top tier applies at its threshold, the others above theirs; separate filers who lived with their spouse use their own table). So the IRMAA ceiling holds a year’s AGI under the threshold of the premium year it sets, grown at the expected inflation, and binds only for a return with someone enrolled by then; in today’s value the earlier AGI is deflated by the expected inflation in between, so today’s-value and inflation-adjusted runs charge alike.

The first two years of a run look back to years before it. Each return sums its members’ earnings history on the Income tab (salaryHistory, pay by calendar year, as entered and not inflated, or the years backtracked from a last salary, see effectiveSalaryHistory) for the lookback year, as an estimate of that year’s AGI; a person with no entry for it counts today’s pay (none once past the salary end age), and the Retirement tab and the Summary warn that the early IRMAA tier is estimated. A household whose income fell because of a life-changing event (retirement, for example) can ask Social Security to use a more recent year’s income (an IRMAA appeal, Form SSA-44); the model does not offer that option.

Compare a conversion with Compare: save one config with the conversion on (a preset sets one ceiling) and one with it off, and pick the two; they run on the same draws (see Using the toolkit).

Roth backdoor

When pay is above the Roth IRA income range, a direct Roth IRA deposit is cut by the phase-out. Switch on Make a backdoor Roth deposit on the Strategies tab to fill that lost room each year: after-tax cash goes into a Traditional IRA as a nondeductible deposit and is converted to the first Roth IRA in the same year. The deposit is the IRA limit (with the catch-up from age 50) times the share the phase-out removes, for each person who earns, and no more than their pay less their Traditional deposit; separate filers each use their own phase-out. With no Roth 401k account the Roth deposit from the Benefits tab lands in the Roth IRA and shares the same IRA limit, so the backdoor gets only what that deposit leaves (the Strategies tab warns when it leaves nothing in the first year). With deposits set per account on the Benefits tab, the person’s Traditional and Roth IRA amounts use that limit instead. An amount per year (today’s value) caps it further. It is paid only from the cash the year leaves before any withdrawal, so it is never funded by selling or withdrawing; it shows as Ret. Deposit (Backdoor) with the other retirement deposits and is not income. The same amount shows as Backdoor Conversion; Roth Conversion holds only the conversions out of Traditional balances, and Conversion Taxable is the taxable part of both.

The conversion follows the pro-rata rule (Form 8606): with no pre-tax money in a Traditional IRA it is tax free; with pre-tax IRA money, the share of the deposit that the pre-tax balance makes up of the whole IRA pool is taxed and shows in Conversion Taxable, and the basis left carries to later conversions and IRA withdrawals. The first year starts from the sum of the IRA basis entered on the Traditional IRA accounts (Accounts tab). The basis is a fixed dollar amount, so in today’s-value runs it loses the expected inflation each year. A Traditional 401k is outside the pool. A Roth IRA account is needed to receive the deposit.

Qualified charitable distributions (QCD)

Charitable giving, entered as Charity rows (an amount per year in today’s value) on the Expenses tab, is charged every year as cash leaving the household (Charitable Giving). Any other Expenses row given the Charity deduction counts as a gift too, charged in its own category. From 2026 a return on the standard deduction also deducts cash gifts up to 1,000 (2,000 joint), and a return that itemizes deducts them as before. A QCD changes how the gift is paid, not the gift.

From age 70.5 (a whole age of 71 or more in the model), switch on Pay gifts with a QCD on the Strategies tab to pay the Charity rows and the rows with the Charity deduction on the Expenses tab straight out of the Traditional IRA. The QCD is all of the year’s giving, or an amount per year in today’s value, never more than the giving, the yearly limit (111,000 per person old enough in 2026, indexed) or what the Traditional IRAs can pay out (the balance at the start of the year less any loss that year). Each person’s part comes only from the Traditional IRAs they own (set on the Accounts tab), in row order (never a 401k), and only while that person is old enough and within their own limit: an IRA with no owner set is yours, so a partner old enough cannot pay a QCD from it. It is a withdrawal that is not taxed (QCD Withdrawal (Non-taxed)): it counts toward the year’s need, so other withdrawals fall, and toward the RMDs of all that person’s Traditional IRAs together (they add up and may be taken from any of their IRAs), never toward the other person’s, so RMDs the QCD covers leave nothing taxable and only the rest of larger RMDs is taxed; a 401k’s RMD is not covered. AGI, taxable Social Security and the IRMAA surcharge two years later fall with it. The gift is still charged in full as Charitable Giving, and the part the QCD pays is not a deductible cash gift, so neither charity deduction counts it. A conversion never uses the QCD or the RMD, and the pro-rata rule leaves the QCD out of the IRA distributions it reads (whether Form 8606 counts it there is unconfirmed). New Jersey does not follow the federal exclusion: it keeps taxing the QCD as pension income, so it counts in the NJ income tests (crossing 150,000 loses the retirement income exclusion) and the exclusion may cover it (a 2006 Division of Taxation position, confirmed only by secondary sources). Every other state follows the federal treatment, which may differ from its real rules.

QCD in RMD years. Once the RMDs start, a QCD is the cheapest way to give: the RMD is taken either way, and the part the QCD covers leaves AGI, so taxable Social Security, the IRMAA tier two years later and the senior deduction’s phase-out can all improve. For a household on the standard deduction it beats the non-itemizer deduction, which caps the deductible gift at 1,000 (2,000 joint). Before the RMD age it still lowers AGI but draws down the Traditional IRA earlier.

Required minimum distributions

The RMD of each Traditional account is the prior year-end balance divided by the distribution period of the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table (Treas. Reg. 1.401(a)(9)-9(c)), held in the federal law file for ages 72 (27.4) through 120 (2.0); ages past 120 use 2.0. It starts at 73, or 75 for someone born in 1960 or later, and is rounded to cents. Each Traditional account uses its owner’s age and birth year (a blank owner is you), so a partner’s account starts at the partner’s own start age, earlier or later than yours; a younger partner’s later RMD years may fall past the plan end, which follows your age. The Joint and Last Survivor Table, which applies when the sole beneficiary is a spouse more than ten years younger and gives smaller RMDs, is not built. RMDs are taken before any other withdrawal and count toward the year’s need; a QCD counts toward them. The read-only Deposits and withdrawals output tab shows each year’s schedule under its chart (see Deposits and withdrawals).

Terminal tax rate

Strategies are compared after tax, by what the beneficiaries keep. Traditional money left at the end of a run is taxed as the beneficiaries withdraw it. Beneficiaries other than a spouse must empty an inherited retirement account within 10 years, so the model spreads each beneficiary’s share over 10 equal yearly withdrawals that empty it while it grows at the accounts’ expected return (net of the mean inflation, balance-weighted). Each withdrawal is taxed at the federal and the beneficiary’s state brackets on top of their other income, at their filing status, with the law year’s brackets held in today’s value (no payroll tax); in today’s value every year’s withdrawal and tax are the same. The inheritance is worth the after-tax withdrawals discounted at the same return, which is the balance less the discounted taxes. Example: one beneficiary, 0% return, 600,000 left: 60,000 a year for 10 years, each taxed on top of their income. That is the default payout, Over 10 years; with All at once a beneficiary takes the whole share in the year after the death, taxed in that one year (today’s value) at the same brackets on top of the same other income, which usually reaches higher brackets: the 600,000 taxed in one year instead of 60,000 in each of ten. For a beneficiary already in the top bracket the two are about equal. Decedents past their RMD age leave the beneficiaries a yearly minimum too; the equal withdrawals are more than it, so they are used either way. Roth money is not taxed. The run ends after the last of the household, so nothing rolls over to a spouse. The beneficiaries are set in the Beneficiaries section of the Benefits tab, one card each (strategies.heirs in a saved config): a name, a share of the estate (the shares must total 100%; an inline error says so until they do), other income, filing status, state (a plain list of the 51 states; Same as household is the state you live in at the end) and payout. The default is one beneficiary with the whole estate, 0 other income, single, Same as household, over 10 years; fields still at their start value are grey until edited, and Add or Remove splits the shares equally again. Each beneficiary is taxed on their own share with their own terms and the taxes are summed; one beneficiary with the whole share gives the same result as before beneficiaries became a list. They set how the after-tax final value is measured; they do not change the plan itself. A typed terminal tax rate replaces the payouts’ tax with that one flat rate on all the Traditional money, whichever the payouts. Investments and homes pass with their basis stepped up to their value at death (IRC 1014), so their gains are not taxed. The homes, cars and loans count, and each home still owned is treated as sold on the last day, less its selling costs (see Summary and deductions). The federal estate tax, 40% on the estate above the basic exclusion (15,000,000 a person in 2026, doubled for a joint return as the survivor keeps the unused exclusion), is charged on the gross estate: the accounts plus homes and cars less loans, before the beneficiaries’ income tax and the selling costs. The exclusion is indexed to inflation: it stays constant in today’s-value runs and grows with each run’s inflation in inflation-adjusted runs. Your home state’s estate tax is charged on the gross estate and its inheritance tax on each beneficiary’s share after the estate taxes, at the rate for children (most states exempt children); the tables are in State estate and inheritance taxes. A state table not flagged indexed is a fixed dollar figure: it stays itself in inflation-adjusted runs and is deflated by the expected inflation to the last year in today’s-value runs, so both modes charge the same tax in today’s value. The federal deduction for state death taxes is not modelled. The beneficiaries’ tax rate box in the Beneficiaries section of Benefits (0% to 50%) taxes the beneficiaries’ payouts while blank and shows the model’s flat estimate as a guide in its caption, read from one seeded run with every strategy off (the IRA basis on the accounts kept), so it never depends on the strategy being judged. Traditional money is taxed when it is taken out, often later and at a higher rate than in the first retirement years, so the estimate follows the trend of the tax rate on Traditional withdrawals: each year’s tax on those withdrawals (Social Security and state knock-ons included) over the taxed withdrawals, for the last 15 years of the run. Years with no Traditional withdrawal are skipped; at least 10 years must have one. A polynomial of degree 1, 2 or 3 is fitted; a higher degree is chosen only when its leave-one-out cross-validation error is at least 10% lower than the lower degree’s, and a curve whose reading differs from the straight line’s by more than 10 points falls back to the line. The trend is read at the last year with a Traditional withdrawal, never beyond the data. With fewer than 10 such years the estimate is the household’s average tax rate on AGI (federal, state and long-term gains tax over AGI, payroll taxes left out) over its first five retirement years. Either way it is clamped to 0% to 50% and rounded to the nearest half point; its caption names the rate and how it was found (the trend’s shape, the ages fitted and its direction, or the years averaged). With no retirement year, no income in those years or invalid inputs, the estimate is 25%. Limits: the run is one seeded run, so its random returns make the yearly rate noisy; there is no survivor or beneficiary rate; a household that spends its Traditional money early gets a low rate on a small balance; and a household with fewer than 10 years of late Traditional withdrawals keeps the first-years average, so the estimate can step when an input change crosses that line. Typing a rate stores an explicit flat rate (terminalTaxRate); Use the beneficiaries’ payout clears it. Outputs, Summary shows Final net worth after terminal tax: each run’s final net worth less the estate and inheritance taxes, the beneficiaries’ income tax on its Traditional balances and the homes’ selling costs, as the median of the runs with their middle half (25th to 75th percentile). The Compare tab uses the first scenario’s saved rate for every row, or with none each row’s own beneficiaries’ payouts (see Using the toolkit).

See also How the home state treats the strategies in Tax law in the model (it used to follow here).

Relocation: moving to another state

A move to another state is a plan of its own (strategies.relocation in a saved config): a switch (enabled), the state you move to (state), your age in the move year (moveAge; blank is your Social Security claim age, or with no benefit the age your pay stops, now when you are already retired; see defaultSpendingStartAge) and whether your homes follow (homesFollow, on by default). It is off by default, and a move to your home state, or with no state, changes nothing. The Relocation section of the Strategies tab, after the QCD, edits them: the Move to another state switch, then while it is on State you move to (the 51 states, labelled like NJ - New Jersey), Move at your age (a blank shows that default age in gray until you type one) and Homes follow. The section’s “i” lists what changes at the move. The guide States: taxes, living costs and moving explains the move for users.

Both of you move at the start of the move year and live in the new state for whole years. From that year:

  • the state income tax, with the new state’s average local income tax, uses the new state’s rules (wmti.taxes.statetax.calculateStateTax() is called with the year’s state, wmti.inputs.retirementInputs.stateInYear);

  • the state you live in during the last year (finalState) sets the state estate and inheritance taxes, and is a beneficiary’s income tax state when their card leaves it blank;

  • your own amounts change only by how the new state compares with the old (wmti.taxes.statecosts): the primary home’s property tax and insurance, the cars’ insurance and registration (the registration by the dollar difference of the states’ car taxes, never below 0), the gas price, a replacement car’s sales tax, rent, the health premium before the Medicare age and the Medicare supplement and Part D premiums after it (inside Health Premium, shown as Health Premium State Change), and the sales tax inside your spending, charged as the signed outflow State Cost Change;

  • with homes following, the primary home is swapped for a comparable one at its value times the states’ typical home value ratio (housing.typical_home_value): the signed outflow Home Equity Change (new value less old), the outflows Move Selling Costs and Move Purchase Costs, and the old home’s gain taxed like a sale. Its property tax also follows the value ratio. The rules are in Moving states.

The legislature catch-up schedule of the state figures is one random stream for every state, so a move changes no random draw: a config with and one without a move compare on the same draws.

Finding the best settings

The Optimize view under Outputs searches the strategy settings for the best score and show it beside your current settings. It is a search on the model, not advice: the result says Not advice; hypothetical, and nothing changes until you apply it.

What is searched. The Roth conversion (off, or one ceiling: a bracket top from 10% to 32% that your filing table has, an IRMAA threshold when a Medicare premium is charged, the 0% gains limit, or a Social Security limit for joint and single filers; then the start and end ages, and in the finer efforts the ages by one year and a second ceiling added to the winning bracket), each person’s Social Security claim age (the benefit table’s ages from 62 to 70), the withdrawal order (the account groups cash, Investments, Traditional and Roth in any order, each group’s accounts in their own order, HSA and 529 accounts in their row, or proportional withdrawals), where surplus cash goes (owner addition: the shares of the cash and Investments accounts, always totalling 100%, first on a coarse grid of quarters, or with more than three accounts each account alone and the even split, then fine steps that move 5% from one account to another; a locked slider keeps its share), and the Roth backdoor and QCDs on or off. The beneficiaries’ payouts are not searched: each beneficiary keeps the payout set in the Beneficiaries section, since taking the money over 10 years always leaves the heirs at least as much as taking it all at once (see known findings). The Relocation state option, first in the list, tries retiring in each state you tick (see States below); with no state ticked it is not searched at all. The conversion’s amount and custom ceiling and the backdoor’s and QCD’s amounts stay as you set them. A conversion that is off starts from the window between your last pay and the year before your required distributions. Not searched: spending, the salary stop age, lowering a deposit, the Traditional/Roth deposit split, returns and healthcare choices, which are life choices more than strategies. A strategy that cannot help the household is skipped with the reason.

401k deposits and new accounts. Six more options, each its own switch and all on by default, let the search save more and open accounts the household lacks:

  • Raise 401k deposits: with the household plan, the Traditional share of pay (tried at 25, 50, 75 and 100% of the gap to the share the 401k limit allows, in half points; only paid people who own a Traditional 401k count toward that share) and, with a Roth 401k, the household Roth deposit (up to the 401k room, without the IRA limit the plan’s own cap would also let it take); with deposits set per account, each Traditional, then Roth, 401k’s deposit up to the maximum its Benefits tab slider shows. Deposits are only raised, never lowered; a person with no 401k is left alone.

  • Add an IRA: for a person who is paid and has no 401k, a Roth IRA or a Traditional IRA (one per person), asking for the room the Benefits tab shows; the model applies the limit, the catch-up and the Roth income phase-out each year. Only with deposits set per account: with the household plan the model puts a plan deposit into an IRA under the 401k limit and sends the Roth deposit to one account, so the option is greyed out (needs per-account deposits) until a deposit slider on the Benefits tab sets amounts per account. With nobody paid it is greyed out (no one has earned pay): IRA deposits need earned income. With the Retirement plan off it is still tried, and adding an IRA also switches the plan on with deposits set per account, so only the new IRA deposits (the Apply text says so); a paid person’s deposit already set on the Benefits tab greys it out instead, as switching the plan on would start that deposit too. A Roth IRA above the income range (pay now, less this year’s Traditional deposits, as the model tests it) is left out (the backdoor strategy covers that). A spousal IRA and the lower Traditional IRA deduction limit when a spouse has a workplace plan are not modeled.

  • Add a 529 per child: for each child under college age (one not born yet included) with a college cost and no 529, a 529 linked to that child and fed from surplus cash. A 529 takes its share only until it holds the college cost it has left to pay and none after the last college year (see the 529 kind on the Accounts tab).

  • Add Savings, Add CD & Bonds, Add Investments: when the household holds no account of the kind and its current settings leave surplus cash in a short probe (three runs on draws of its own), an account of the kind fed from surplus cash.

A new account starts at $0 with the kind’s usual return and risk, is named <Kind> (added) (529 <child> (added), a partner’s IRA <Kind> (added, partner), with a number when the name is taken) and is placed last in its withdrawal group. The search adds at most two accounts at once on Quick and three on Standard and Thorough, one per step, and the finer efforts also try removing one; a new account that receives surplus takes an equal part of what locked shares leave, and one left with under 1% of the surplus is dropped. Its returns come from draws of its own, the same in every candidate, so the other accounts’ draws stay as they were; an added Roth IRA that replaces its owner’s zero-value rollover account takes that account’s draws instead. When nothing can be added, the search is exactly the search without these options; when something can, it gets more runs, time and steps, so it can end elsewhere even if it adds nothing. A later run of the saved scenario can differ slightly from the search’s numbers. An option that cannot do anything for your settings is unticked and greyed out with its reason on both pages (optimizeAvailability), and the search lists it as skipped. Coordinate descent can miss an IRA that pays only together with another change.

Relocation state. The Relocation state option is a list of the 51 states (NJ - New Jersey, with a search box above it on the phone) to tick, up to 5 per search; once five are ticked the others are off and the note Up to 5 states per search. shows, and None clears the ticks. Each ticked state is tried as a retirement move, at the move age under Strategies, Relocation (your Social Security claim age when it is blank), with staying in your home state always a candidate; your current Relocation plan, if any, is the starting point. The state is the first step of each pass, so the other strategies are then tuned inside the chosen state, and the next pass checks the choice again. Every ticked state reaches the second round of runs (the round keeps the best six, not a third). Ticking only your own state skips it (pick at least one other state), and with a blank move age that falls after the last year it is greyed out. Under the list the page shows what the ticked states add, as in 3 states and staying put: up to 672 more runs, up to 2 minutes more (estimate), timed on your device (see the estimates). The result names the state, for example Retire in Florida, move at 65 or Stay in NJ, and adds a note that a move is valued on taxes and the costs that follow you, not on climate, family or healthcare access. Apply turns Relocation on with the best state (the move age stays as set), or off when staying put wins over a planned move. See Letting the optimizer compare states for what a move changes.

Filing status (couples). For a household with a partner, the Filing status (couples) option (on by default) tries filing jointly and filing separately; without a partner it is greyed out (no partner). The status is one setting for the federal return and every state return alike. It changes the tax of every year, so it is searched right after the state and before the Roth conversion, the claim ages and the rest, which are then tuned inside the status it picks (the built-in finding filingStatusEarly, see known findings). Separate returns often cost more federal tax: more of Social Security is taxed, the brackets are narrower and several limits are lower. Some states, though, let each spouse claim their own exclusions or caps (a pension exclusion that applies per return, for example), so the search weighs both. Income is not split between the spouses beyond what the model already does for separate returns: each account’s income goes on its owner’s return and a joint account’s is shared (see the Owner column on the Accounts tab). The result row reads, for example, Filing status (couples): Joint, Separate, and Apply writes the filing status into the inputs like the other settings.

The score. Each run’s value is its after-tax final worth in today’s value, what the beneficiaries keep: the Traditional money taxed by the beneficiaries’ payouts as set, or charged the terminal tax rate you set the same way for every setting, and the federal and state estate taxes and the inheritance tax. The score is:

  • the typical outcome: the runs sorted by value and averaged with weights that fall with the distance from the median run (a Gaussian taper in percentile rank, sigma 0.20): the middle run counts fully, a run at the 25th or 75th percentile about half as much, one at the 10th or 90th about a seventh, so a few huge outcomes cannot pull it;

  • plus a success bonus: the share of runs with no year of unpaid bills that do not run out, times a weight, times the current settings’ typical outcome (at least 100,000);

  • less a spread penalty: a weight times the distance between the 10th and the 90th percentile, so a wider spread costs score even when the extra width is upside.

Same draws and the re-check. Every setting faces the same random draws, run by run, so the difference between two settings comes from the settings. The search moves one strategy at a time with the others held (coordinate descent) and compares the candidates of each step in rounds: all of them on a few runs, the best third (at least two) and your current pick on more, the best two on the most. A new setting replaces the current pick only when its score is higher by more than half a percent of the reference. The winner is then run again, with your current settings, on one or two fresh sets of draws; the result says whether it held up. One that did not is likely luck; it can still be applied, with a warning.

Effort. Quick (6, 14 and 30 runs a round, one pass, at most 650 runs and 6.5 minutes), Standard (8, 24 and 80 runs, two passes with the finer steps, at most 2,500 runs and 25 minutes) and Thorough (16, 48 and 160 runs, three passes, at most 9,000 runs and 90 minutes). A search that may add accounts gets 300, 900 or 2,500 more runs (180, 540 or 1,500 more seconds), one that may raise 401k deposits 200, 500 or 1,200 more (120, 300 or 720 seconds), and each ticked state 50, 224 or 672 more (the passes times one candidate’s whole round of runs, 6+14+30, 2 x (8+24+80) or 3 x (16+48+160); 30, 134.4 or 403.2 more seconds), so five states on Standard make at most 3,620 runs under a 36-minute cap; Continue at the budget still adds the effort’s own budget. The app starts on Quick. Each time cap is three times the budget at 0.2 s a run, a safety stop; a slow device on a large profile can reach it (a desktop took 0.02 to 0.4 s a run on the sample and pinned profiles), and the estimate says so. Stop shows the best so far; Cancel keeps nothing. A search that uses its run budget before finishing asks The search used its N runs before finishing. Continue with another N runs?: Continue carries the same search on from where it paused with the budget raised by the effort’s budget (no setting is run twice, and the result is the one a search started with the larger budget finds; the time spent waiting does not count toward the time cap), asking again at each new budget until the search finishes; Stop and keep best so far shows the best so far and re-checks it on fresh draws.

Estimates. Each Effort label says how long a search should take on your device, as a range, for example Quick (estimate 25 s to 40 s). Until your settings’ options are known it shows the run budget instead (Quick (up to 650 runs)). The runs come from the options your settings allow (optimizing.searchEstimates): the low end counts, exactly, the runs of one pass in which no step changes anything, which is also what a search that finds nothing better takes (stepCosts); the high end allows for steps that do change something, since a change opens later steps (a conversion’s age window, say) and calls for another pass, plus the re-check, held under the run budget. The seconds are those runs times a short timed calibration: two runs of your current settings on your device. The search keeps every run it has made, so a later pass does not run a candidate again when nothing else changed since it last ran: on the sample and pinned profiles a second pass took 5% to 75% of the first, and a search whose first pass changed nothing stopped after it. While a search runs, the time left is the time per run measured so far times the runs its remaining steps are expected to take: the rest of this pass (after the first pass only the steps another change made new), another pass only once this one has changed something, and the re-check only once the best differs from your settings; it is still held under the time cap. It grows when a step finds something better and shrinks when one does not. The measurements behind these numbers are in docs/planning/optimizer-estimate-accuracy.md.

Limits. Coordinate descent finds a local best: a better setting that needs two strategies to move at once can be missed (the second pass catches some). 30 to 80 runs resolve only larger gains, so a small real gain may read did not hold up. No one dies in the model, so a later claim and conversions are valued as if both of you live to the end of the run. Every setting is judged after tax the same way and runs on the same fixed draws. To keep the draws the same, every setting runs with the zero-value rollover account a conversion adds; settings applied with the conversion off are saved without it, so a later run of them can differ slightly.

The result. The result shows one card per recommended change: the setting, its value now and the value found, and the score that change added when the search took it (each accepted step’s winner less the incumbent on that step’s runs, summed over the change’s steps, so with the settings as they were then; the gains need not add up to the total improvement shown under the cards). Only changes that improved the score are recommended. The settings kept fold into one line, N other settings kept as they are, that opens on a tap.

Apply. The result also lists, folded, each setting Apply writes. Apply these settings changes the settings at once (not when your settings changed since the search) and Undo takes them back. Only the settings the search moves can be written, and only as the search moves them: a deposit or the plan’s share only goes up, an existing account keeps every field but its surplus share (and a raised 401k deposit), a new account starts at $0 and is one of the kinds above, a child’s 529 link only fills a blank one with a 529 that exists, and the States search writes only the Relocation switch and state.

Known findings. General results about the search, true for any household, are kept in .config/optimizerFindings.json (read by wmti.strategies.findings) so that a search does not spend runs re-learning them. Each finding has an id, a statement (what holds), a basis (how it was measured or reasoned, with the date), an effect on the search (its kind and details), a status, a confidence (owner ruling, measured, reasoned or unconfirmed) and a check_at date. The status says how it is used:

  • applied: the optimizer acts on it. The one kind it acts on is removed: the dimension named by effect.dimension is never searched (a dimension the optimizer still builds is left out and shown as skipped with the finding’s id). The Beneficiary payout was removed this way (heirsPayoutSpread, owner ruling 2026-10-23): spreading over 10 years always leaves the heirs at least as much, and leaving it out kept every winner while a Quick search of the pinned profiles ran 30 fewer runs (800 to 770, 500 to 470 and 740 to 710).

  • built-in: a rule the optimizer’s code already holds, such as skipping a claim age for someone already claiming or the backdoor without a Roth IRA; recorded so every rule is in one place.

  • hypothesis: recorded with what was measured and never applied, such as searching the dimensions with the largest typical gain first, or not trying claim ages before 65: each saved runs on one pinned profile but changed the winner on another.

To add a finding, add an entry with every field to the findings list, with the measurement or the reasoning and its date in basis. Record it as applied only when its effect is a kind the optimizer acts on and a test shows the search keeps the same winner (or a better score) on the pinned profiles in fewer runs (see backend/tests/strategies/test_optimizer_findings.py); otherwise record it as a hypothesis with the measured difference. A malformed file is refused with the field to fix. The file is part of the code version, so a saved search made before a finding changed is refused rather than resumed. When a finding’s check_at date has passed it is re-checked: python -m wmti.strategies.findings lists the due ones, and each is measured or reasoned again and its basis, status and check_at updated.

Limitations of the strategies

  • No one dies in the model, so the survivor’s single brackets and surcharge thresholds, the main reason a married couple converts, never apply; the value of a conversion is understated (the tab warns).

  • No 10% early-withdrawal penalty and no Roth five-year rules.

  • Account owners set ages, QCDs, conversion targets, Traditional deposits and, on separate returns, whose return taxes the account’s income; but the after-tax IRA basis (Form 8606) is one household figure, so the taxed part of the Traditional income is spread over the returns by what each owner’s accounts paid out; the household plan’s Roth deposit and the backdoor deposit go to the first Roth account whoever earns them (deposits set per account land in their own accounts).

  • No Net Investment Income Tax, so a conversion that lifts income above its threshold costs less here than it would.

  • No IRMAA appeal and no ACA premium credit.
