States: taxes, living costs and moving

Where you live changes more than your income tax. Property tax, insurance, car costs, sales tax and the price of gas all differ from state to state, and they can add up to more than the income tax itself. This page explains what WMTI knows about each state, how sales tax is handled, what happens when you plan a move at retirement, and how the optimizer can compare a few states for you.

You choose your state as the home state on Setup (see The app); every state list in the app (home state, a beneficiary’s state, the state you move to) shows the 51 choices as code and name, such as NJ - New Jersey. The tax rules behind it are in Tax law in the model and Model rules by group; this page is the friendly tour.

Which states, and what each one holds

All 50 states and Washington, DC are supported. Each one has its own file in the law data (.config/law/<state>.json, for example fl.json), so correcting a state means editing one file; adding one also lists it in .config/law/states.json (see The list of states). Each file holds:

Item

What it means for you

Income tax brackets

The state’s own rates and brackets, deductions and exemptions, for each filing status. States with no wage tax have a single 0% bracket.

Average local income tax

County, city and school-district income taxes (Maryland counties, New York City, Pennsylvania and Ohio towns and so on) averaged into one rate for the state and added to the state tax. Where the local tax reaches wages only (as Pennsylvania’s earned income tax does), it is charged on the pay alone, even below the state’s deduction, and interest, retirement income and capital gains are left out of it; otherwise it is charged on the income the state taxes. States without local income taxes have none.

Social Security, pensions and IRA withdrawals

Whether the state taxes your Social Security (not at all, the same part the federal return taxes, or only above an income limit), and how much pension and IRA income it leaves out (many states exclude some or all of it for retirees).

Property tax

An effective rate: the typical yearly tax as a share of the home’s value.

Home and car insurance

Typical yearly premiums for a home and for a car with full coverage.

Car taxes

Registration plus any yearly vehicle property tax for a typical car, and the sales tax rate charged when you buy a car: the state’s combined general rate, except in states with a vehicle tax of their own (Alabama, Mississippi, North Carolina, Virginia and West Virginia among them).

Gas tax and pump price

The state’s tax per gallon and the average price at the pump.

Electricity

The average home electricity price, in cents per kilowatt-hour.

Typical home value

What a typical home in the state is worth, used to compare states fairly.

Estate and inheritance tax

The state’s estate tax (on what you leave) and inheritance tax (on what your beneficiaries receive), if it has either.

Sales tax

The state and average local rates, the rate on groceries and whether clothing is taxed (see Sales tax).

How sure are the numbers? Every item carries a confidence label in its file (confirmed primary, confirmed secondary or unconfirmed), and every file a status (final or estimate); Confidence chips and Status: final or estimate in How to read the law pages define both. Each item also has a check_at (where to check it) and a note. The table in The state law files gives each state’s status, its weakest income-tax label and the rules that are approximated. The living-cost figures (property tax, insurance, car costs, gas, electricity, home values and sales tax) all come from secondary research: compilers such as the Tax Foundation, the Census, the Missouri cost-of-living index, insurer quote sites, the federal energy agency and Zillow. They are labelled that way in the files, and they are averages, not quotes for your home or car.

Reading each state’s law. Every state’s file (and the federal one) has its own page in this documentation, under Tax law by jurisdiction: every section the model reads, as the file states it, with each section’s confidence label as a colored chip, its sources and notes, and rates shown as percents beside the stored fractions (How to read the law pages explains them). The pages are written from the files, so they always show the figures the simulation uses. In the app, the menu’s Documentation opens this guide, and the See this state’s rules link under the home state and the Relocation state opens that state’s page (see Documentation in The app).

Each state file also holds three sections from a second research pass:

  • health_premiums: monthly list prices per person, without subsidies, of the benchmark marketplace (ACA) silver plan at 60, Medigap Plan G at 65, the average Part D drug plan and the average Medicare Advantage plan (KFF, quote sites and CMS files). Most are unconfirmed. A figure the research could not find, or found doubtful, is left empty (null): DC has no Medigap figure, and Alaska, Massachusetts, North Dakota and Vermont have no Medicare Advantage figure.

  • price_indexes: the Missouri grocery index and the federal Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) price levels of goods and of services other than rent, each with the US at 100. After a move they re-price your monthly expense rows (see Moving at retirement below).

  • long_term_care: state median monthly costs of a home health aide, assisted living and a private nursing home room (CareScout, confirmed primary). Stored for reference only; the model does not use them.

Sales tax

Your expenses already include sales tax. When you type in what you spend, the receipts you are copying from already have your state’s sales tax in them. WMTI does not add sales tax on top: that would count it twice.

Which spending is taxed: your expense rows. Sales tax does not apply to everything you buy. Housing, insurance, most healthcare and most services are not taxed, and many states exempt groceries or clothing. So WMTI looks at each row on the Expenses page: each row is either taxable or not taxable, and only the taxable rows count.

  • A row you add yourself is taxable.

  • Each suggested row has its own default (set in .config/expenseCategories.json): goods such as Household supplies, Electronics and Gifts are taxable, and so are the services most states tax (Dining out, Wi-Fi & cable, Cell plans, Subscriptions, Entertainment, Gym, Travel). Prescriptions, Out-of-pocket medical, Education, Life insurance, Family support and pure services (Personal care, Home repairs, Pets, Lawn & cleaning, Car repairs, Transit & rideshare) are not.

  • Groceries follow the state’s own rate on groceries (0 where they are exempt, a reduced rate in some states, plus local taxes where towns tax food), and Clothing follows the state’s rule on clothing (taxed at the combined rate, or not at all in states such as New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Minnesota).

  • The Home, Cars and Healthcare pages, rent and Charity rows never count here.

To mark a row, open it on the Expenses page and set Taxable to Taxable or Not taxable; Default (shown in gray) goes back to the row’s default. Many states tax few services, so a row of your own that is mostly labor (a cleaner, a tutor) is worth marking Not taxable.

For each taxable row the tax inside it is:

sales tax = amount x rate / (1 + rate)

with the rate of the state you live in that year: the combined rate (state plus average local), the grocery rate for Groceries, and 0 for Clothing where the state exempts it.

Worked example. You spend 1,000 a month on taxable rows (no groceries or clothing) where the combined rate is 5%, and move to a state where it is 8%:

  • the tax inside it now: 12,000 x 0.05 / 1.05 = about 571 a year;

  • after the move the same goods cost 12,000 x 1.08 / 1.05 = 12,343, about 343 a year more, of which 914 is tax.

Your untaxed rows (rent, insurance, medicine) do not change.

Shown for records, never added. The Taxes chart and the tax tables show how much sales tax is already inside your spending, so you can see it. It appears as a hatched (striped) bar and as the last table column, labelled Sales tax (already in expenses, shown for records); the year-by-year Summary has a Sales Tax (records) column. Compare shows the median lifetime sales tax under the same label, and the PDF report’s Taxes chart and yearly taxes table mark it the same way. These are never added to your total tax, your outflows or any total: the money is already counted in your expenses.

Moving at retirement

Many people plan to move when they stop working. The plan is the Relocation section of the Strategies tab, after the QCD (strategies.relocation in a saved config): switch on Move to another state, pick the State you move to (each state is listed by code and name, such as FL - Florida; on a phone, type in the Search box above the list to find it) and set Move at your age. The move age shows your Social Security claim age in gray until you type your own (with no Social Security benefit, the age your pay stops, or now if you are already retired); clearing it goes back to that age. The walkthrough’s optional Tax strategies asks “Plan to move to another state at retirement?” and sets the switch and the state, then, when you own a home or rent, “Does your home move with you?” (Home follows me). Both of you move together, at the start of the move year, and you are treated as living in the new state for the whole year.

What changes from the move year. Your own numbers stay the starting point; WMTI changes only the parts a move changes, by comparing the new state with the old one:

  • Income tax: from the move year your state tax (and the average local tax) is worked out with the new state’s rules, including how it treats Social Security, pensions and IRA withdrawals.

  • Your home: the move adds a primary residence in the new state to your Home timeline, one that sits at the same place against the new state’s typical home value as yours does against your current state’s. It is worked out each run from the strategy; your saved homes never change. The switch Home follows me (on by default, under Move at your age) says what happens to the home you live in before the move:

    • On (your home follows you): it is sold at the close of the year before the move, at its value then, with its own selling costs; the mortgage is paid off from the proceeds and the rest goes to your accounts. Its gain is taxed like any home sale, with the home sale exclusion, by the state it sits in (you still live there that year). At the start of the move year a comparable home is bought in the new state: its price is your home’s value then (in today’s value) times the new state’s typical home value over your current state’s (housing.typical_home_value in the state files), and it grows at your rate from there; it pays your home’s closing cost and transfer tax rates and fees in cash (Asset Purchases); its property tax is your entered tax times the new state’s rate over the old one’s and times the value ratio, and its insurance is scaled by the states’ average premiums. Your mortgage carries over: the new home borrows what the old one still owed, at the same rate, extra payment and months left, so the Loan Balance goes on from where it was. Its cost basis is its price with the transfer tax and fees; a sale within two years of the move gets no exclusion.

    • Off (your home stays where it is): the old home is kept as a second home, with its own value, costs, mortgage and any sale you set, taxed under its own state’s rules when sold (a second home gets no home sale exclusion). The new primary residence is bought as above, financed like a new purchase (20% down over 360 months at your old rate) when the old home has a mortgage, else in cash.

    • Renting: the rent period you are in ends and a new one starts in the move year in the new state, at your rent times the new state’s housing-cost index over your current state’s, to the end of the old one. On and Off are the same here.

    • A home or rent period of yours that starts in the move year is the move itself: nothing is added (a blank state on it becomes the new state; another state you set wins, with a warning). With no home and no rent in the move year, nothing is added and only the state moves. A move in the first year of the plan re-bases the home you own now in place (the comparable home, no sale or purchase).

  • Car costs: car insurance is scaled the same way, each car’s registration and car taxes change by the difference in dollars between the two states’ typical yearly car taxes (see Car taxes below), and a replacement car bought after the move pays the new state’s car sales tax.

  • Gas: the gallons your car already uses cost the difference between the two states’ average pump prices more (or less) per gallon.

  • Everyday prices: each monthly Expenses row (Bills, Planned Spending and Other Spending) follows the new state’s price level by its price type. The suggested rows carry one from .config/expenseCategories.json: Groceries follow the grocery index; goods rows (clothing, household supplies, furniture and appliances, electronics, gifts) the BEA goods price level; services rows (dining out, Wi-Fi and cable, cell plans, personal care, home and car repairs and the rest) the BEA services price level; rows priced the same everywhere (subscriptions, travel, prescriptions, life insurance) do not change. A row you add yourself has no price type and follows a mix of 40% goods and 60% services, the shares of household spending outside housing and healthcare in the BEA consumer spending figures (the weights are in the same file). Major Spending and Charity rows never change. Worked example: 800 a month of groceries in New Jersey (grocery index 107.6) after a move to a state with an index of 90 costs about 800 x 90 / 107.6 = 669 a month there.

  • Sales tax: the taxable rows the price levels do not re-price (taxable rows priced the same everywhere, such as Subscriptions and Travel, and Major Spending) cost the new state’s sales tax instead of the old: amount x (1 + new rate) / (1 + old rate) (see Sales tax). Rows that are not taxable, the Home, Cars and Healthcare pages and rent take no sales tax change. The price levels are shelf prices that already hold each state’s sales tax, so a re-priced row takes no separate sales tax change: each dollar changes once. The price change and the sales tax change are charged together as one line, State Cost Change, in your cash flow; moving somewhere cheaper makes it negative (a saving).

  • Health premiums: the health premium you pay before the Medicare age is scaled by the new state’s marketplace (ACA) premium at 60 compared with your current state’s; from the Medicare age, the Medicare supplement premium by the Medigap Plan G premium and the Part D premium by the Part D premium. Medicare Part B and the income-related surcharge (IRMAA) are federal and do not change. The change is part of Health Premium and shows on its own in the Summary column Health Premium State Change. A state with no figure (empty) leaves that premium unchanged. Worked example: a 70-year-old paying 250 a month for a supplement in New Jersey moves to Florida, where Plan G averages 359 a month against New Jersey’s 201, and pays about 250 x 359 / 201 = 447 a month there.

  • Car taxes: each car’s registration and car taxes per year become your entered amount plus the new state’s typical yearly figure less your current state’s, in today’s dollars, never below 0. The figures are registration plus any yearly vehicle property tax for a typical car; where the state’s figure was not researched, it holds at least the state’s flat passenger-car registration fee (a floor, so the state’s real cost may be higher). Worked example: 300 a year in New Jersey (figure 65) becomes about 300 + 1,156 - 65 = 1,391 a year in Virginia, whose cars pay personal property tax, and 300 + 48 - 65 = 283 a year in Pennsylvania (flat registration).

  • Any other cost: a cost the old state’s file holds as 0 or leaves empty stays unchanged.

  • Estate and inheritance tax: the state you live in at the end of the plan is the one whose estate and inheritance taxes apply, and by default your beneficiaries’ state too.

What stays anchored to your own numbers. Your salary, Social Security and pension amounts, your account balances and returns, your entered spending (apart from the sales-tax difference), any second home, and any home or rent period you add that starts after the move keep exactly what you entered; your housing changes only as described above. For a sale and purchase at prices of your own, add them on the Home timeline (a home or rent period starting in the move year is the move), and your entered numbers rule. Utility bills are not re-priced (see Limits and assumptions).

With Relocation off, or with the new state the same as your home state, nothing changes at all.

Moving through your homes. Your Home timeline can also say where you live: a primary residence you buy later, or a rent period that starts later, with its State set moves you to that state from its first year, with the same changes as above for income tax, cars, health premiums, prices and sales tax (your own amounts for that home or rent are kept as entered). Leave the state blank and you stay where you were; with no home and no rent for a while you stay in the last state; a second home never moves you. Every cost is compared with your home state, so moving twice, or back home, needs nothing more. A Relocation move and moves through your homes apply in year order; a home or rent period that starts in the Relocation move year decides that year. You live in one primary residence at a time: a primary home and a rent period may share only their move year.

Letting the optimizer compare states

The Optimize view (see Finding the best settings) has a States section, first in its list of options: the states as NJ - New Jersey, with a search box on the phone. Tick the states you would consider, up to 5 per search (a firm limit: the search runs every strategy again in each state, so more states take a lot longer). Once five are ticked the others are off and the page says Up to 5 states per search.; None clears the ticks.

  • It runs each one. Each ticked state is tried as a retirement move, alongside staying where you are, which is always a candidate. The state choice is searched first, then the other strategies are tuned inside the chosen state, and the next pass checks the choice again. Ticking only your own state does nothing; the search says to pick at least one other state. A move age after the last year of the run greys the section out until you set an earlier one under Strategies, Relocation.

  • More states, more time. Before you tick any, the page says Tick the states you would consider; staying where you are is always compared. Then it shows an what the states you ticked add to the chosen effort, for example 3 states and staying put: up to 672 more runs, up to 2 minutes more (estimate). on Standard, the time from runs timed on your device. Each ticked state adds the search’s passes times one candidate’s full ladder of runs to the budget (Standard: 2 x (8 + 24 + 80) = 224 runs; Quick 50, Thorough 672) and those runs at three times 0.2 s each to the time cap (Standard about 134 s per state).

  • Later homes go with the state tried. Homes and rent periods that start at or after the move are set to the state tried; earlier ones keep the states you set, and Home follows me stays as you set it. Staying put restores your states.

  • The result names the best state, for example Retire in Florida, move at 65 (with ; later homes set to Florida when a later home or rent period gets that state) or Stay in NJ, with its score beside your current settings. The move age is the one under Relocation (your Social Security claim age when blank). Applying it fills in the Relocation section for you: Relocation on with the best state and the later homes’ states written, or off when staying put beats a move you had planned.

How this differs from Relocation. Relocation is your own plan: one state and one move age that you choose, and every run uses it. The States section is a search: it tries several states for you and reports which one scores best on after-tax money left for your beneficiaries. Like the rest of the optimizer, it is a search on the model, not advice: it does not value climate, family, healthcare access or anything else that makes a place right for you.

Why a no-income-tax state can cost more

“No income tax” sounds like a big saving, but for many retirees the state income tax is small: most states do not tax Social Security, and many leave out a large part of pension and IRA income. The costs that do not stop at retirement, property tax, insurance, gas, electricity and sales tax, can matter much more.

Reading it. Florida, Texas and Washington have no income tax, yet each costs this household more than Pennsylvania, which does: Florida’s home insurance is the highest in the country, Texas has high property tax and insurance, and Washington has high home values and sales tax. Pennsylvania, New Jersey and New York leave most retirement income untaxed, so their income tax for this couple is close to zero.

A caveat. These are illustrative estimates from secondary research, for one example household and a buyer at the typical price; they are not quotes and they are not what WMTI will show for your plan. They leave out car taxes, senior property tax relief and homestead caps (such as Florida’s Save Our Homes, California’s Proposition 13 and the Texas over-65 freeze), which can cut property tax a lot for people who already own their home. A single filer, or a household with more taxable income, would pay more income tax in states such as California and New York. Run your own numbers, with your own home, car and spending, before deciding.

How state law is structured, and adding a rule kind

For developers. A state’s income tax is computed by classes in wmti.taxes.staterules, built from its file; no class names a state, and every number stays in the file. Each kind of section has a class derived from LawSection (BracketSchedule, StandardDeduction, Exemptions, ExemptionCredits, RetirementExclusion, SocialSecurityRule, GainsExclusion, LocalIncomeTax, EstateInheritanceTax, StateBase and StateStrategies), and each state gets one StateLaw of the family its brackets choose: NoIncomeTaxState (a single 0% bracket), FlatTaxState (one bracket per filing table) or GradedTaxState.

To add a new kind of rule:

  1. A rule many states share (a new kind of section): add the section and its checks to the law files and taxdata, write a LawSection subclass that reads it (its SECTION key, the method that applies it and notes for the dashboard), give StateLaw an attribute for it and call it from the hook it changes (or add a hook to StateLaw.calculate, keeping the order), and add its sentence to NOTE_ORDER.

  2. A rule one state needs that the sections cannot express: subclass the state’s family, override the one hook that differs, say why in its docstring, register it with @registerRuleClass("<name>") and set "rule_class": "<name>" at the top of that state’s file. The factory refuses a name that is not registered, and a class whose fits rejects the state’s brackets.

  3. Add unit tests on a small synthetic law dict, and check that every other state’s tax is unchanged (backend/tests/taxes_law/test_staterules.py compares each state with state_law_snapshot.json).

Limits and assumptions

  • Local income taxes are averages. County and city income taxes are averaged over the whole state, so a move within a state, or to a high-tax city such as New York City, is not captured exactly.

  • Some Social Security rules are approximated. A few states (for example Colorado, Minnesota, Montana, Utah and Vermont) have rules that do not fit WMTI’s three patterns. They are approximated and flagged unconfirmed in the state’s file and in the State rules notes.

  • Insurance comes from one source. Home and car insurance averages come from a single quote site, and such sites disagree by 20 to 40%. WMTI uses them only to compare states (as a ratio), never as your premium.

  • Utilities are not modelled. Electricity prices are kept for comparing states, but your utility bills are not re-priced after a move.

  • Not modelled either: senior property-tax relief and homestead caps, part-year residence, marketplace premium subsidies (the health premium is scaled at list prices), and the price of food, goods and services in your expense rows. The Tax law in the model page lists what the model leaves out.
