Tax law in the model

Purpose: for readers who want to know which tax rules the model applies, how the home state treats the strategies, and which rules it leaves out.

The strategies are in Strategies; every law figure is on the law pages (Tax law by jurisdiction). This is a model of the law, not tax advice.

How the home state treats the strategies

Only New Jersey has state-specific treatment: a Roth conversion is retirement income, so the NJ exclusion can cover it once you qualify, and a QCD stays taxable (see Qualified charitable distributions (QCD)). Every other state follows the federal treatment: a conversion is taxable and retirement income, a QCD is left out. The senior and non-itemizer deductions never reach a state return. The Strategies tab shows the note for your home state; it is the model’s reading, not a confirmed one.

State estate and inheritance taxes

What beneficiaries keep (see Terminal tax rate) pays the estate tax of the state lived in at the end of the plan on the gross estate, and its inheritance tax on each share after the estate taxes, at the rate for children (2026 figures). Thirteen jurisdictions have an estate tax (CT, DC, HI, IL, MA, MD, ME, MN, NY, OR, RI, VT, WA) and five an inheritance tax (KY, MD, NE, NJ, PA); the others have neither. Four examples:

Social Security, local income tax and living costs

  • Social Security. Most states exempt it. Colorado and Montana tax the part the federal return taxes. Connecticut, Minnesota, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah and Vermont exempt it below an income limit. A rule none of these fits (Colorado, Minnesota, Rhode Island, Utah, Vermont) is approximated and marked unconfirmed.

  • Local income tax. A state average: 0 for NJ and FL, about 1% of wages in PA, about 1.5% of all income in NY (New York City residents pay about 3.5%, others nothing).

  • Living costs (sales tax, property tax, insurance, car taxes, gas, electricity, home value, housing cost index). Compiled secondary sources; car taxes are rough composites marked unconfirmed. A cost the file holds as 0 stays unchanged after a move.

The list of states

.config/law/states.json lists the 50 states and DC by two-letter code and name. It is not a law file. Each state file’s name must match its entry, and a listed state with no law file is refused. An entry may carry loader rules for that state alone. No state list or per-state rule is written in the code.

Law backlog

These rules are not charged by the model yet.

Rule

What is left out and why it matters

Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

A 3.8% federal tax on the lesser of net investment income and income above 200,000 single, 250,000 joint or 125,000 separate (not indexed). A conversion can pull interest and gains into it; charging it changes results for households with large taxable accounts.

Section 125 payroll-tax savings

A pre-tax employer health premium also escapes Social Security and Medicare tax; the model still charges them.

Credits and the alternative minimum tax

No tax credits (such as the child tax credit) and no AMT.

Early-withdrawal penalty and the Roth five-year rules

There is no 10% penalty before 59.5, so the Roth rules have no effect.

Affordable Care Act premium credit

The enhanced credits expired after 2025: from 2026 income above 400% of the poverty level loses the whole credit. The Strategies tab warns with the household’s figure; the credit itself is not priced.

Rules that change in a given year

A law section can start or end in a given year (the senior deduction ends after 2028), but cannot switch to new figures, such as new brackets, New York’s rates or the IRMAA top tier. The SALT cap is the exception: it grows 1% a year through 2029 and reverts to 10,000 in 2030.

Social Security benefit calculation

Each table amount is the benefit at its claim age; ages not entered are estimated on straight lines. Earnings history (Retirement tab, Benefit comes from) uses a simplified method in today’s dollars. Not modeled: the year of eligibility (earnings indexed to age 60), cost-of-living adjustments, each past year’s own wage base, the earnings test, the special minimum and a recomputation for earnings after the claim. The 40 work credits are approximated by ten years with earnings. WEP and GPO were repealed.

Spousal, survivor and other family benefits

Each person gets only their own benefit: no spousal, survivor (no deaths), divorced-spouse or child benefit.

Social Security earnings test

Benefits claimed before full retirement age are not withheld while earning above the annual limit, so an early claimant who still works is overpaid.

Joint-life RMD table

Only the Uniform Lifetime Table is used; a spouse more than ten years younger as sole beneficiary would give smaller RMDs.

IRMAA appeal

A life-changing event (such as retirement) lets Social Security use a later year’s income for the surcharge; the model always looks back two years.

Survivor and mortality

No one dies in the model, so a survivor’s single brackets and surcharge thresholds never apply; a conversion’s value is understated, and a later Social Security claim is judged to the end of the plan horizon.

Federal itemized-deduction limits for 2026

A 0.5% of income floor on charity and a 35% cap on the value of itemized deductions are to be checked when the deductions feature is built.

State rules that do not fit the schema

Not modeled: the federal income tax deduction some states allow (Alabama, Missouri, Oregon), state-only capital gains taxes (Washington’s excise, Maryland’s 2% surtax), phase-outs at high incomes, part-year residence, senior property-tax freezes and homestead caps, Medicaid and long-term-care rules, and enacted rate changes for later years. Local income taxes are one statewide average. Approximated rules are named in The state law files.

Figures marked estimate have not been checked against a primary source; see the status line of each law file.
