Tax law in the model

For readers who want to know which tax rules the model applies and which it leaves out: how the home state treats the strategies, then the law backlog of rules not charged yet. The strategies themselves are in Strategies. Every figure of every law file, federal and for each state, with its confidence and sources, is on the law pages (Tax law by jurisdiction). Everything here is a model of the law, not tax advice.

How the home state treats the strategies

Only New Jersey has state-specific treatment, from the state_strategies section of its law file. A Roth conversion is taxed there as retirement income, so the NJ retirement income exclusion can cover it once you qualify; a QCD stays taxable as retirement income although the federal return excludes it (see Qualified charitable distributions (QCD)). NY, PA, FL and any other state follow the federal treatment: a conversion is taxable income and retirement income, a QCD is left out. The senior and non-itemizer deductions never reach a state return, which starts from income before federal deductions. The Strategies tab shows the note for the home state you pick; it is the model’s reading of the rules, not a confirmed one.

State estate and inheritance taxes

What beneficiaries keep (see Terminal tax rate) pays the estate tax of the state lived in at the end of the plan (the home state, or the Relocation state after a move) on the gross estate and its inheritance tax on each beneficiary’s share after the estate taxes, at the rate for children. The figures are each state law file’s estate_inheritance section, for 2026. Thirteen jurisdictions have an estate tax (CT, DC, HI, IL, MA, MD, ME, MN, NY, OR, RI, VT and WA) and five an inheritance tax (KY, MD, NE, NJ and PA; Maryland has both); the others have neither. Each section carries its own confidence label and check_at page. The table below gives the four original states as examples; the other files’ notes describe theirs.

Social Security, local income tax and living costs in the state files

Each state file also holds how the state taxes Social Security (social_security: exempt, federal or threshold), an average local income tax rate (local_income_tax.avg_rate and whether it reaches only wages or all income) and the living costs a move between states changes: sales tax, property tax, home and car insurance, car taxes, gas tax and pump price, electricity price, typical home value and a housing cost index. Most states exempt Social Security; Colorado and Montana tax the part the federal return taxes (federal), and Connecticut, Minnesota, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah and Vermont exempt it below an income limit (threshold). The local rate is a state average, for example 0 for NJ and FL, about 1% of wages in PA, and about 1.5% of all income in NY (New York City residents pay about 3.5%, everyone else nothing). A Social Security rule the three treatments cannot express (Colorado, Minnesota, Rhode Island, Utah and Vermont) is approximated and marked unconfirmed. The living costs come from compiled secondary sources (confirmed secondary, electricity confirmed primary for January to July 2026); car taxes are rough composites marked unconfirmed, and a state whose file holds 0 for one leaves that cost unchanged after a move.

The list of states

.config/law/states.json lists the jurisdictions the model knows: the 50 states and DC, each a USPS code and a full name, under a schema_version (1). It is not a law file. wmti.taxes.taxdata reads it once at import into ALL_STATE_CODES and STATE_NAMES; stateName answers from it, and each state file’s header name must match its entry. An entry may also carry the loader rules for that state alone: required_sections (New Jersey’s file must have exemptions) and brackets_with_over (New Jersey’s brackets each give over). The loader refuses the list, naming the field, when a code is not two upper-case letters, a code or name is listed twice, or a listed state has no law file. No state list or per-state rule is written in the code.

Law backlog

These rules are not charged by the model yet. Each is tracked, with its status, in the project notes; the model’s results leave them out.

Rule

What is left out and why it matters

Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

A 3.8% federal tax on the lesser of net investment income and income above 200,000 single, 250,000 joint or 125,000 separate (not indexed). A Roth conversion raises income and can pull interest and gains into it. Left out because charging it changes results for households with large taxable accounts.

Section 125 payroll-tax savings

A pre-tax employer health premium also escapes Social Security and Medicare tax; the model still charges them.

Credits and the alternative minimum tax

No tax credits (such as the child tax credit) and no AMT are modelled.

Early-withdrawal penalty and the Roth five-year rules

There is no 10% penalty before 59.5, so the Roth rules have no effect.

Affordable Care Act premium credit

The enhanced credits expired after 2025: from 2026 income above 400% of the poverty level loses the whole credit. The Strategies tab warns with the household’s figure (aca_premium_credit in the law data); the credit itself is not priced.

Rules that change in a given year

A law section can start or end in a given year (the senior deduction ends after 2028), but cannot switch to new figures, such as new brackets, New York’s rates or the IRMAA top tier. The SALT cap is the exception: its own fields grow it 1% a year through 2029 and revert it to 10,000 in 2030.

Social Security benefit calculation

The benefit is entered by default: each table amount is the benefit at that claim age, already reduced or increased, and the ages not entered are estimated on straight lines, not from a full-retirement-age amount. It may instead be estimated from the earnings history (Retirement tab, Benefit comes from: Earnings history), by the simplified method in today’s value: not modelled are the year of eligibility (earnings indexed to age 60 and that year’s bend points) and cost-of-living adjustments, each past year’s own wage base (this year’s caps every year), the earnings test, spousal and survivor benefits, the special minimum and a recomputation for earnings after the claim; the 40 work credits are approximated by ten years with earnings; WEP and GPO were repealed.

Spousal, survivor and other family benefits

Each person gets only their own benefit: no spousal benefit (it needs the worker’s primary insurance amount, which is not stored), no survivor benefit (no deaths), no divorced-spouse or child benefit.

Social Security earnings test

Benefits claimed before full retirement age are not withheld while earning above the annual limit, so an early claimant who still works is overpaid.

Joint-life RMD table

Only the Uniform Lifetime Table is used; a spouse more than ten years younger as sole beneficiary would give smaller RMDs.

IRMAA appeal

A life-changing event (such as retirement) lets Social Security use a later year’s income for the surcharge; the model always looks back two years.

Survivor and mortality

No one dies in the model, so a survivor’s single brackets and surcharge thresholds never apply; the value of a conversion is understated, and a later Social Security claim is judged to the end of the plan horizon.

Federal itemized-deduction limits for 2026

A 0.5% of income floor on charity and a 35% cap on the value of itemized deductions are to be checked when the deductions feature is built.

State rules that do not fit the schema

Each state’s own income tax is its brackets, deductions, exemptions or credits, a retirement exclusion and one of three Social Security treatments. Not modelled: the deduction of federal income tax some states allow (such as Alabama, Missouri and Oregon), state-only taxes on capital gains (Washington’s excise, Maryland’s 2% surtax), phase-outs of exemptions, credits and deductions at high incomes, part-year residence, senior property-tax freezes and homestead caps, Medicaid and long-term-care rules, and state rate changes already enacted for later years. Local income taxes are one statewide average. The rules that are approximated instead are named in The state law files.

Figures in the law files marked estimate have not been checked against a primary source; see the status line of each file.
