Model rules by group

For readers who want the rules behind each screen: this page says, group by group, what the model does with what you enter and what the shipped example household holds. The app describes the screens themselves. Defaults quoted here are those of the shipped default profile (backend/wmti/inputs/default_profile.json) and the input classes in wmti.inputs.retirementInputs. The strategy rules are in Strategies and the law backlog is in Tax law in the model; this page links there instead of repeating them.

Money is shown in one of two ways. By default it is in today’s value, so a figure from a year far ahead buys what the same figure buys now. With the inflation adjusted switch on (Setup), figures are inflation adjusted instead and grow with each year’s drawn inflation. The Inflation section of Using the toolkit explains how the two ways relate. A figure marked (%) is a percent: type 3 for 3%.

Household and run

What it is. Setup’s Household & run, Run and Inflation cards: the household, the run settings and the inflation adjusted switch with the inflation range. Everything here applies to the whole run, whichever tab is shown.

Defaults. The default profile has a partner, files JOINT, lives in NJ, runs 65 years, is in today’s value.

Logic. A saved scenario is never changed by anything but your own edits (or by a merge or Make B match A on Compare). With inflation adjusted money the model draws inflation each year from the Inflation range on Setup and grows expenses, Social Security and the law’s indexed figures with it. The figures the law does not index (state brackets, the Social Security taxability thresholds, Medicare Part B, the SALT floor and the mortgage limit) are assumed to keep up with inflation too; a scenario saved with that assumption turned off keeps it, but no screen changes it. In today’s value it instead deflates returns and pay by the mean of that range, so the two ways tell the same story. The seed makes comparisons fair: two scenarios run on the same draws differ because of their settings, not luck.

Each year the model takes the larger of the standard deduction and the itemized deductions; the year-by-year figures are on Outputs, Summary. The state starts from income before federal deductions. The state treatment of each strategy is under How the home state treats the strategies.

Accounts

What it is. Every account the household holds, how withdrawals are ordered, each account’s return, cost basis and IRA basis, and where leftover cash goes.

Defaults. The default profile (fictional round figures) has five accounts: Savings (25,000), Investments (50,000), a Traditional 401k (100,000), a Roth 401k (100,000) and CD & Bonds (0), with PRIORITY withdrawals and a surplus split of 33%, 33%, 0%, 0% and 34%.

Logic. A Traditional account’s RMD and an IRA’s QCD follow its owner’s age, a Roth conversion lands in a Roth account of the same owner, and each person’s Traditional deposit and employer match go to their own Traditional account (else to the first one). Filing separately, an account’s interest, investment gains and taxed withdrawals (RMDs and conversions included) go on its owner’s return; only a JOINT account’s are split by the partner’s share of joint accounts and shared items on Setup. Turning off Include a partner makes you the owner of the partner’s accounts. Only the part of an Investments withdrawal that is gain, the share of the balance above the cost basis, is taxed, as long-term capital gains. Returns are drawn each year from the mean and standard deviation; see Strategies for how withdrawal order interacts with tax (the choices that change a plan). A saved scenario with owners does not load in an older checkout of the toolkit (it fails on the unknown owner field).

Income

What it is. Pay, raises and past earnings for you and, when included, your partner. The current ages are on Setup; Social Security is on Retirement.

Defaults. The default profile has you and your partner both age 35 with salaries of 100,000 and 80,000 that stop at age 60, a raise of 3.3% mean and 0.3% std dev and a 25% yearly chance of a 10% promotion raise. The earnings history is on Manual entry and empty; a profile saved before Last salary existed loads on Manual entry. Nobody is retired, and a profile saved before the I’m retired switch (version 34 or older) loads with it off and no typed years, so its results do not change.

Logic. Each year’s raise is the mean plus the standard deviation times a normal draw; the year’s pay factor is 1 plus the raise plus any promotion raise, never below zero (pay does not turn negative). A file saved when the raise was a minimum, most likely and maximum loads with the same mean and spread as that old range (its exact triangular mean and standard deviation), rounded to the slider steps of 0.1% and 0.05%. A Last salary history is computed each time from the last salary and the raise inputs and never stored, so changing a raise refills it; only the years typed over it are stored (salaryOverrides). Earlier pay is backtracked from age 22, a fixed assumption of the tool, not an SSA rule. A retired person’s pay stops at today’s age whatever Salary stops at age holds, for the paycheck, the commute, the employer match, the dependent care FSA and the Social Security estimate alike.

Children

What it is. The children, one card each, with their daycare and college costs in a section each, and how much each child raises the ordinary recurring expenses in its child years. The dependent care FSA is an employer benefit, on Benefits.

Defaults. Two children, Child 1 and Child 2, born when you are 33 and 36, each in their child years up to age 20, and a child inflation factor of 20% per child. A child’s college starts at 18 for 4 years and its daycare runs from age 0 to 5, both with no cost, so none is charged.

Logic. The child years follow your age, so a child’s years stay put when the run’s calendar changes. In a year with n children in their child years the ordinary recurring expenses are multiplied by 1 + n times the factor; n is also the number of dependents the state exemption uses (see Summary). A scenario saved before the child rows (version 16 or older) loads with one identical row per child, born at the old Child years from your age and in the child years for the old window’s length; as every end age is inclusive, the year you reach the old window’s end counts too.

A child’s college cost is charged in Major Spending in your ages from your age at birth plus the college start age, for the college years, grown by inflation like the other rows; a child already in college pays only the years left. The College Costs summary column shows that part of Major Spending; it is not added to the expenses a second time. The cost is an education cost: a linked 529 pays it first, up to its balance, then the rest of every education cost is drawn from the 529 balances left, by the withdrawal strategy, and the usual accounts pay what the 529s cannot. A scenario saved before the college fields (version 17 or older) loads with no college cost, so its results do not change.

A child’s daycare cost is charged the same way, in Major Spending, in your ages from your age at birth plus the daycare start age up to and including your age at birth plus the daycare end age, grown by inflation. It is not shown on Expenses and not saved as an expense row. It counts as dependent care: it is added to the rows tagged Dependent care in the Tagged Dependent Care summary column, which caps the dependent care FSA. A scenario saved before the daycare fields (version 19 or older) loads with no daycare cost, so its results do not change.

Home

What it is. The timeline of when you own each home and when you rent; the homes you own or will own, with their values, mortgages, property taxes and insurance, an optional purchase or sale during the run, and which one is your primary residence; the rent periods, each with its own rent; the household’s utilities; and the Mortgage loans that do not fit a home’s mortgage figures.

Defaults. One home named Home, the primary residence, with a value of 0, an appreciation equal to the mean inflation (3.33%: the mean of 1%, 3% and 6%), no mortgage and property taxes of 6,000 (a new home starts with the same appreciation); no rent periods and no utilities. The default profile has no loans; its housing payment is a Bills row on Expenses. A new home bought on the timeline is a primary residence with a 20% down payment, 3% closing costs, no transfer tax, 6% selling costs and no penalty.

Logic. Each home’s property tax is charged each year in the Property outflow (inflation adjusted like the expenses) and counts as state and local taxes, every home under the one SALT cap; each home’s insurance is charged in Bills. A home’s value in a year is its value today times (1 + its appreciation) for each year. As for an account’s return, with inflation adjusted dollars off the mean inflation comes off it: the growth is (1 + appreciation) / (1 + mean inflation) - 1, so a home appreciating at the mean inflation keeps its value today. With them on the value grows at the appreciation itself, whatever inflation each year draws. A home bought later starts at its price at that year’s prices. The sum is the Home Value summary column and counts in net worth (the loans still owed, the mortgages included, are subtracted). With its switch on and a balance above 0, a home’s mortgage is a Mortgage loan named after the home, charged, deducted and subtracted from net worth as described under Loans. Each rent period’s monthly rent times 12 is charged in Bills in each of its years, inflation adjusted and not raised by the child inflation factor; a year with no home and no rent period charges no housing. The primary residences and the rent periods are one lane, one at a time: a primary home and a rent period that share a year other than a move year (one sold or ended in the year the other starts) are refused, naming both and the ages; a rent period beside a second home is allowed. A primary residence bought later or a rent period starting later with a state moves you to that state (see Moving states under Strategies).

The timing rule of Cars applies: a home is bought at the start of the year you are its purchase age and sold at the end of the year you are its sale age (at its start when the next primary home or rent period starts that year). A home bought later has no value, property tax, insurance or mortgage before its purchase year; its value starts at its price and grows by its appreciation from then. In the purchase year the down payment (the whole price without a mortgage), the closing costs, the transfer tax and the fees are paid in cash in Asset Purchases; with the Mortgage switch on the rest is a Mortgage loan that starts that year and counts toward the deduction like any home’s mortgage. A home sold has its value at 0 from its sale year and its property tax and insurance stop the year after; renting after it is a rent period on the timeline. The sale price is the one entered, or with 0 the home’s value that year before the sale. The selling costs (Selling Costs) and the mortgage still owed, with its prepayment penalty, are taken from the price; the rest is Asset Sale Proceeds, deposited to the cash and Investments accounts by the surplus rule, while a mortgage above the price is paid from the accounts in Loan Payoffs.

The gain on a sale is the price less the selling costs less the cost basis (the one entered, or the value today for a home owned now, so only growth from now is a gain, or for a home bought later its price with the transfer tax and the fees; the closing costs stay out). A primary residence’s gain is excluded up to the section 121 limit of the law data (250,000 single, 500,000 joint, 250,000 per separate return, split by the partner share; figures unconfirmed), assuming you owned and lived in it 2 of the last 5 years, except that a primary bought during the run and sold less than 2 years later gets no exclusion. The rest, and the whole gain on any other home, is a long-term capital gain of the return (federal, state and the knock-ons on the taxable Social Security and the deductions), shown as Home Sale Gain, with the exclusion used as Home Sale Exclusion. A loss is not deducted. The state tax on the gain is that of the state the home sits in, since a gain on real property is source income of its state even for a non-resident: the home’s own state (Home.state), else the state lived in when it was first held (the home state for a home owned now; the comparable home a Relocation move buys is in the new state, below). When that is not the state lived in that year, the gain is left out of the year’s state income and taxed alone with the home’s state’s rules (the gain is that state’s whole income for its brackets), added to State (wmti.simulation.model._sourceTax). This is an approximation: no credit from the state lived in for the tax paid to the other state and no part-year apportionment. Depreciation recapture, the net investment income tax, the partial exclusion and rental income are not modelled. Example: a primary residence worth 500,000 today, 2% appreciation, sold at the end of the year you are 59 with 6% selling costs and 1,000 of fees, with no basis entered: the gain is the price less the costs less 500,000, excluded up to the limit. A home still owned at the end is not sold in the run; only the Summary’s Final net worth after terminal tax and Compare’s after-tax columns treat it as sold on the last day, by the beneficiaries: they pay the selling costs but no gains tax, since an inherited home’s basis steps up to its value at death (see Terminal tax rate).

The mortgage interest deduction counts the primary residence and one other home: each year the other home with the most mortgage interest (the first on a tie), and the Other mortgage loans with the primary residence, all under the one principal limit of the law data (half of it for each separate return). A third home’s mortgage interest is not deducted, so the primary residence changes the deduction only with three or more mortgaged homes; primary residences held in turn count as one. Home equity debt used for anything but the home, the higher limit for debt from before 2018, mortgage insurance premiums, utilities per home and rental income are not modelled; a second home’s utilities and upkeep go on Expenses.

A scenario saved before the homes list loads with one primary home holding its home value, property taxes and home insurance, and its first Mortgage loan that starts now with no refinance or forgiveness moves onto that home (Mortgage switched on); its other Mortgage loans stay under Other mortgage loans. A scenario that rented loads with no homes, its Mortgage loans kept; a home value, property taxes or insurance it held, which counted for nothing while renting, are dropped with one warning. Results do not change. A scenario saved before the purchase and sale switches (version 30 or older) loads with them off. A scenario saved before the appreciation was read like a return (version 31 or older), when it was the growth above inflation r, loads with (1 + r) x (1 + its own mean inflation) - 1: in today’s value its home values stay the same; with inflation adjusted dollars on its homes now grow at that fixed rate instead of each year’s drawn inflation. The timeline reads the saved purchase and sale ages, so every saved scenario shows its homes as they were. A scenario saved with one household rent (version 34 or older) loads with a rent period for each run of years in which no primary residence was held, at that rent (“Rent”, “Rent 2” and so on; renting today when the run starts there, open when it reaches the end), so the rent is charged in the same years and results do not change. A scenario saved before the rent periods had a state (version 50 or older) loads with none, so its results do not change; one saved with a primary residence bought later whose state is not the state lived in at its purchase now moves you there from its purchase year, and one warning says so.

Cars

What it is. The household’s cars: each car’s value and depreciation, its loan or lease, its commute, its running costs, an optional replacement cycle and when it is bought, sold or leased during the run, all in today’s value, and the fuel price for every car.

Defaults. No cars, and a fuel price of 3.50. A new car, from Cars or the walkthrough, is driven by you, loses 20% of its price in its first year (the usual first-year drop, first_year_drop in .config/carDepreciation.json) and 10% of its value a year after, has its Loan and Commute switches off (with a 7% rate, 60 months and 230 commute days ready once they are on), 25 miles per gallon, and no replacement (first in 5 years once a cycle and a price are set). It is owned, not leased (a lease payment of 0), and a car bought on the timeline has a 10% down payment and no sales tax, fees, selling costs or prepayment penalty. The default profile keeps its Car, Car Insurance, Car Maintenance and Gas/Tolls rows on Expenses.

Logic. A car owned today is defined by its Purchase price (as paid), the price paid in the dollars of the purchase, and its Year bought. The depreciation calculator gives its value today: the price times (1 - the first-year drop) times (1 - the depreciation per year) for each year owned after the first (wmti.cars.cars.estimatedValueToday); a car bought this year or later is at its price. Depreciation first year is the car’s own first-year drop; blank, it shows (gray) and uses the usual one from .config/carDepreciation.json (20%, a typical new car’s first-year loss in Carfax and Edmunds figures, confidence: unconfirmed). The car’s sheet shows the result gray in its one Value today field. A figure entered there wins over the estimate; blank or 0 (its reset button) uses it, and with neither a value nor a year bought the car has no value. A profile saved before the year bought (version 48 or older) keeps each car’s value today, now the override, so its results are the same. From today the value falls by the depreciation per year each year, after the first-year drop when the car was bought this year; a car with no year bought is taken as past its first year. A car bought later, and each replacement, starts at its price and loses the first-year drop in the year after the purchase, then the depreciation per year. The value is inflation adjusted like the home value; it is shown as the Car Value summary column and counts in net worth. Fuel each year is (2 times the commute miles one way times the commute days while the driver works, plus the miles per year) divided by the miles per gallon, times the fuel price; tolls and parking are charged per commute day while the driver works. A car that does not commute has no commute days, so only its miles per year count. The commute stops when the driver’s salary stops (their Salary stops at age); the miles per year, insurance, registration, maintenance and the loan run on. A replacement buys the same car again, repeating to the end of the run or the sale year, by the rule Replace by selects: Years, every so many years (Replace every (years), the first after First replacement in (years)); Miles, every so many miles (Replace every (miles)); or Both, whichever comes first, each replacement restarting both counts. The rule not selected is ignored, though its figures are kept; a selected rule at 0 never replaces (Years with 0 years: no replacement). A new car replaces by Years; a file saved before the setting takes Both when both of its rules are above 0, Miles when only the miles rule is, else Years, so its results are the same. The miles are the car’s commute and other miles per year since the last purchase, a year’s miles counting at its end, so 12,000 miles a year and 36,000 miles replace the car at the start of year 3; a car owned today starts from Miles on it today (a car bought later from 0). The price is the replacement price, or with 0 the purchase price (the same model; for a car owned today, the price paid, as paid and not grown to today’s dollars), which the blank Replacement price shows gray; with neither, the car is not replaced. The Cars Timeline shows each replacement in the year the run makes it, labelled by age, by miles or by age and miles (both rules met that year), and gray as calculated; it is display only. At each replacement the car is traded in at its value and the new one is paid by Replacement paid by (blank: the car’s own How it is paid). Paid in cash, the rest of the price, the sales tax and purchase fees on the price and the selling costs on the trade-in are paid in Car Replacement and the car’s loan runs on. Financed, the down payment less the trade-in’s value (at least 0) with the same tax, fees and selling costs is paid in Car Replacement; the car’s loan is paid off at the trade-in (at the close of the year before, with its prepayment penalty, in Loan Payoffs) and the price less the larger of the down payment and the trade-in’s value is a new Auto loan from that year at the replacement loan rate, term and down payment (blank: the car’s own), named after the car: “Sedan loan 2”, “Sedan loan 3” and so on, so two loans of one car never overlap. Either way the value then starts again from the price. Leased, the car’s loan is paid off, its value is a sale (Asset Sale Proceeds, less the selling costs) and from then the car is leased: 12 lease payments a year in Car Lease, the lease’s down payment and fees in Car Replacement at each lease start, no value and no sale; each lease runs its term rounded up to whole years, then the next starts, sooner when the miles rule is met first. Example: a car replaced every 36,000 miles at 12,000 a year and paid by lease is turned in at the start of year 3, and a 24-month lease starts again in years 5, 7 and so on. A profile saved before the pay method keeps its replacements paid in cash. The price is in today’s value, so it grows with inflation in a nominal run and stays flat in today’s value. All costs grow with inflation like the other expenses and are charged in the one Car Costs outflow (shown in the Expenses chart), with Car Fuel, Car Tolls, Car Upkeep, Car Replacement and Car Lease as its parts. Financed (How it is paid: Finance), a loan balance above 0 is an Auto loan run by the loan engine like a loan on Loans: its payments are in Loan Payments and its balance is subtracted from net worth. No car cost is tax deductible. Removing the partner makes a PARTNER car yours.

Ages are your ages, and one timing rule serves every purchase and sale: a car is bought at the start of the year you are its purchase age and sold at the end of the year you are its sale age, so the sale year is still charged; when the next car of its lane starts in that year, the sale is at the start of the year, booked at the close of the year before, and only the new car is charged that year. A car bought later has no value, costs, commute or loan before its purchase year; its value in the purchase year is its price, then falls by the first-year drop and the depreciation, and its replacement cycle counts from the purchase. In the purchase year the down payment (the whole price without a loan), the sales tax and the fees are paid in cash in the Asset Purchases outflow, from the accounts in the withdrawal order like any expense (a large purchase can raise the tax on Traditional withdrawals that year, or show as Shortfall (unpaid)); with How it is paid Finance the rest is an Auto loan that starts that year, and with cash the whole price is paid. A car sold has its value, costs, commute and replacements stop: its value is 0 from the sale year and its costs from the next year. The sale price is the one entered, or with 0 the car’s value that year before the sale (today’s value, the purchase price or the latest replacement’s price after the first-year drop and the depreciation since). The selling costs (Selling Costs, shown for information) and the car loan still owed after the sale year’s payments, with its prepayment penalty, are taken from the price; what is left is Asset Sale Proceeds, income that the surplus rule deposits to the cash and Investments accounts by their surplus shares, while a loan above the price is paid from the accounts in Loan Payoffs. A car sale is not taxed, gain or loss. Example: a car bought at 55 for 30,000 with 10% down, 6% sales tax and 500 of fees takes 3,000 + 1,800 + 500 in cash that year (inflation adjusted) and borrows 27,000.

A leased car’s purchase and sale years are the lease’s first and last years. In each year of the lease Car Lease charges 12 lease payments (inflation adjusted), and its fuel, insurance, registration and maintenance are charged as for an owned car; it has no value, no loan, no replacement, no purchase cash and no sale proceeds. A payment due at signing, a residual or a buyout is not modelled. Example: a lease of 400 a month from 2031 to 2033 adds 4,800 a year (inflation adjusted) to Car Costs in those three years.

A scenario saved before cars had their own group loads with no cars and the default fuel price, so its results do not change; its car rows stay on Expenses until you move them. A scenario saved before the switches (version 27 or older) loads with each car’s Loan switch on when its loan balance is above 0, and its Commute switch on when its commute days are above 0 and its commute miles or its tolls and parking are above 0, so its results do not change. How it is paid (Cash, Finance or Lease) is one setting that also sets the car’s Loan and Lease switches (Car.payMethod, hasLoan, leases). A scenario saved before it (version 49 or older) loads with each car’s pay method set to what the car did: lease while leased, a car bought later its pay method (blank: finance with the Loan switch on), a car owned today finance with its Loan switch on and cash with it off, the Loan switch on for finance only; a blank Replacement paid by that followed a different pay method takes that method, so every replacement is paid as before and its results do not change. The warning about car rows on Expenses still reads the figures as entered, switch or not. A scenario saved before the purchase and sale switches (version 29 or older) loads with them off, so its results do not change. A scenario saved before the timeline (version 33 or older) loads with no lease, and its cars get lanes in list order so cars held at the same time are on different lanes; a lane changes no result.

Loans

What it is. The household’s loans other than the mortgages and the car loans, with extra payments, a refinance, forgiveness, a later start or an early payoff.

Defaults. The default profile has no loans; its housing payment is a Bills row on Expenses. A new loan starts now and runs to its last payment (both switches off, no penalty).

Logic. Payments come out of cash flow as expenses, and the balances are subtracted from net worth. The mortgage interest deduction comes only from Mortgage loans, from their interest and balance each year, with the limits in the law data; Auto, Student and Other loans never count toward it. When the payment is already a row on Expenses (such as a Mortgage row in Bills), tick Payment in Expenses: the loan is then not charged and not taken from net worth, and it only feeds the deduction; otherwise the payment is charged twice.

With Starts later on, a loan owes nothing and costs nothing before its start year, and its balance is the amount borrowed then; no cash arrives with it, so a loan that pays for a car or home purchase belongs on that car or home. With Pay off early on, the loan makes its payments through the end of the year you are its payoff age (not before it starts), and the balance then still owed is paid in one lump sum from the accounts, plus the prepayment penalty, in the Loan Payoffs outflow; payments and interest stop and the balance is 0. The penalty is not deductible. A loan whose payment is an Expenses row cannot be paid off early, nor can a forgiven one (a write-off with no cash, a different case). Example: a loan paid off at the end of the year you are 56, six years from now, charges that year the balance after its 84th payment times 1.02 with a 2% penalty. A scenario saved before the switch (version 29 or older) loads with Starts later on for each loan with a start year above 0, so its results do not change.

Benefits

The Benefits group holds the retirement plan, the medical plan with its HSA, the dependent care FSA and the beneficiaries.

Retirement plan

What it is. How the household saves into 401k and IRA accounts each year, and each person’s employer match.

Defaults. Off for a new household. Each person’s employer match is on for a person with an active salary (salary above 0 and a salary end age above their age) and off otherwise, with a rate and cap of 0; a new partner starts as a copy of you. The default profile has a retirement plan with a Traditional deposit of 10% of pay, no Roth deposit and both matches on at 0.

Logic. Each person’s Traditional deposit and employer match go into the first Traditional account they own (a 401k before an IRA), else the first Traditional account; the Roth deposit goes into the first Roth account. The yearly limits come from the law data. Each slider runs from 0 to what the limits leave at the current ages: a person’s Traditional and Roth 401k share the 401k limit, their Traditional and Roth IRAs the IRA limit (two Traditional IRAs share one), each with the catch-up from its age; the 401k deposits plus the employer match stay within the total-additions limit (IRAs and the catch-up are outside it); and every deposit stays within the person’s own pay, so a partner with no pay has no room (a spousal IRA is not modelled). Raising one slider lowers the most the others sharing its limit can take; an account with no room says why. Each amount lands in its own account, and the employer matches each person’s 401k deposits and the match goes to their own Traditional 401k, else Traditional IRA. A Traditional IRA deposit is treated as deductible, and the Roth IRA income limit cuts a Roth IRA deposit each year (the slider maximum leaves it out, and a note appears when your income is in that range). The Traditional-versus-Roth trade-off is described under “Traditional or Roth saving” in the choices that change a plan. A saved scenario with account amounts does not load in an older checkout of the toolkit.

Medical plan

What it is. What the health plan costs and how it shares the cost of care. Doctor visits, drugs or a flat healthcare amount are on Healthcare, and Medicare and IRMAA under the Medicare heading of Retirement. Premiums paid after tax and the healthcare costs the HSA did not pay count toward the medical deduction by themselves; other medical costs are rows with the Medical and dental deduction on Expenses.

Defaults. Off. The premium is zero; a 1,500 deductible, 20% coinsurance and a 6,000 out-of-pocket maximum, per person. These are placeholders, not sourced.

Logic. The health premium before the Medicare age is charged as spending in today’s value and grows 1.5% a year above general inflation, like every healthcare cost and premium (a fixed figure, not an input). A premium paid before tax lowers taxable pay; one paid after tax can be itemized above the medical floor. A pre-tax premium is taken from pay, so each return’s part of it is held to that return’s wages less its Traditional deposits and dependent care FSA; the rest (all of it in a year without pay) is paid after tax and itemized like an after-tax premium. The Pre-tax Premium column is the part the pay covered. Without a medical plan the visits before the Medicare age are paid at their full price, with no deductible, coinsurance or maximum.

HSA

What it is. A health savings account: pre-tax money that pays healthcare costs in the same year.

Defaults. Off, with a contribution of 0.

Logic. The contribution, in today’s value and grown by inflation, is held to the law’s limit (family while a partner is under the Medicare age, with the catch-up) and to the year’s healthcare cost. It lowers adjusted gross income and adds no cash outflow of its own, since the cost it pays is already charged; the healthcare cost it did not pay counts toward the medical deduction.

Dependent care FSA

What it is. Pre-tax pay set aside for child care, an employer benefit.

Defaults. Off, with an amount of 0.

Logic. The FSA sets aside, each year, the smallest of its amount (grown by inflation), the year’s dependent care (the children’s daycare cost and the rows tagged Dependent care: the Tagged Dependent Care summary column) and the federal limit, and only in years with a child under 13 that is in its child years. The amount is split between the returns by pay; each return is held to the federal limit for its filing status (7,500, or 3,750 filing separately, not grown by inflation; the figures are unconfirmed in the law file) and, with a partner, to the lower of your two pays. It lowers the pay that federal and state income tax, Social Security tax and Medicare tax are charged on, so MAGI and those taxes fall, while Gross Income and Paycheck stay as they were; the Dependent Care FSA summary column shows the amount. It takes no cash: the care is already charged in full, so the cash gained is the tax saved. Every state follows the federal treatment. The dependent care credit (Form 2441), and a student or disabled spouse, are not modelled; the Roth IRA income limit and the Social Security earnings record use pay before the FSA. A scenario saved before the FSA (version 18 or older) loads with it off and no row tagged, so its results do not change.

Beneficiaries

What it is. Who inherits what is left and how they take it, with the terminal tax rate. They set how the after-tax final value is measured (the tax they pay when they inherit Traditional money); they do not change the plan itself.

Defaults. One beneficiary with the whole estate, no other income, filing single, in the state you live in at the end, taking the money over 10 years; no terminal tax rate.

Logic. Each beneficiary has a name, a share of the estate (the shares must total 100%), other yearly income (today’s value), filing status, income tax state (Same as household is the state you live in at the end) and payout (over 10 years or all at once). Each is taxed on their own share of the Traditional money with their own terms, and the taxes are summed; the inheritance tax is charged on each one’s share. A scenario saved before the list (version 44 or older) loads its number of heirs as that many beneficiaries with equal shares and the same terms, so its results do not change. Details: Terminal tax rate.

Healthcare

What it is. What healthcare costs, which always count in the expenses: one flat yearly amount or range (Flat amount, the default), or doctor visits and drugs (Visit-based); Medicare is on Retirement. The health premium, the HSA and the plan’s cost sharing are on Benefits; without a medical plan there, the visits before the Medicare age are paid in full. Drugs are an expense here, not part of the plan.

Defaults. Flat amount, 0 at both ends (no cost until you set one, like the other expenses). For Visit-based: 1, 4 and 10 visits a person a year, 150, 300 and 1,500 a visit and 60 a month in drugs; all placeholders, not sourced.

Logic. With Visit-based, each person pays the visits and drugs every year: through the health plan’s cost sharing before the Medicare age, and through Medicare’s from it (see Medicare). The costs are in today’s value and grow 1.5% a year above general inflation.

With Flat amount, the household pays one level every year: with a range, it is drawn once per run, evenly between the two ends, and kept for every year, so the Monte Carlo runs show the uncertainty. The single run is one seeded draw like any other run, not the midpoint. The level grows with general inflation plus 1.5% a year, like the visit costs. It ignores the Medicare age and the supplement’s share of the costs (the supplement premium is still charged), and it is part of the Healthcare column, so the HSA pays it and it counts as a medical expense. A scenario saved before the flat amount loads Visit-based with its results unchanged.

A scenario saved with its own growth above general inflation (on the premiums, the visits or the old age table) loads without it, with a warning; its costs then grow by the fixed 1.5%. A scenario saved with the old healthcare cost by age table loads without it: healthcare costs are included when it had either healthcare costs or visit-based costs on. One that had only visit-based costs on keeps its results; one that charged the age table now pays the visits and drugs instead, with a warning. A scenario saved with long-term care figures loads without them; one with healthcare costs on that charged long-term care loses that cost, with a warning.

Expenses

What it is. The household’s expenses that no other group enters, plus the random major expense. The inflation range is on Setup’s Inflation card.

Defaults. A random major expense of 0, 5,000 and 50,000. The default profile’s rows (generic names and fictional round amounts) are examples to replace. A new scenario and the default profile also hold the suggested monthly rows of .config/expenseCategories.json (suggestedExpenses), each at 0 with its price type (ExpenseItem.priceType: grocery, goods, services or none); a row at 0 changes nothing, and the price type matters only after a move (see the Relocation rules). A saved profile is never given the suggested rows.

Logic. Gifts to charity are rows of the Charity category: they are charged each year as their own outflow (Charitable Giving), count toward the charity deductions and are what a QCD pays (see Strategies). They are never raised by the child inflation factor. The From and To ages follow your age.

A scenario saved before the To age existed loads with any age on a Bills, Planned Spending or Other Spending row cleared: the model never read it there, so the results do not change.

A row with a deduction is still charged as spending exactly as before; the deduction only adds what the model charges for it that year (monthly rows times 12, raised by the child inflation factor in the child years unless the row carries the older Exclude from child inflation tag; a row only between its From age and To age; inflation adjusted like the row) to that deduction, which then follows the law for its category, in years the return itemizes:

  • Medical and dental counts only above 7.5% of AGI. Premiums paid after tax, Medicare Part B, the supplement, IRMAA and the healthcare costs the HSA did not pay on Benefits and Healthcare already count, so tag only other medical costs (dental, vision, hearing, costs outside the plan).

  • State and local taxes count within the SALT cap together with the state income tax and each home’s property tax on Home, which already count, so do not tag a row that repeats them. The cap is 40,400 in 2026 and grows 1% a year through 2029 (in the year’s own dollars, whatever the fixed-figures setting); from 2030 it reverts to 10,000 (5,000 separate) with no phase-down.

  • Mortgage interest counts in full: the principal limit scales only the Mortgage loans on Home, by their own balance. Tag only the interest part of a payment, and do not tag the payment row of a Mortgage loan, which counts already.

  • Charity counts within 60% of AGI less the 0.5% floor, toward the non-itemizer charity deduction, and a QCD can pay it, like a Charity row.

Filing separately, each return takes its share of a tagged row, as with the other shared household items.

A scenario saved before the deduction inputs moved loads with its charitable giving as a Charity row, its mortgage interest and balance as a Mortgage loan used for the deduction only, and its random shocks dropped (use the Random major expense or Major Spending rows). A scenario saved with medical expenses per year (in the old Healthcare group) loads without them: they repeated costs Benefits and Healthcare already count. A warning names what moved or was dropped.

Retirement

What it is. Social Security for each person (under a You and a Partner sub-heading), then Medicare and, on Original Medicare, the Medicare supplement, then Spending in retirement, each under its own heading. The earnings history the Social Security estimate uses (entered or backtracked from a last salary) is on Income.

Social Security

What it is. Each person’s benefit basis (Earnings history or Manual entry), benefit table and claim age. Earnings history pays the estimate from earnings at every age from 62 to 70; Manual entry pays from the benefit table, one row per claim age from 62 to 70, with the monthly benefit your Social Security statement shows for that age (in today’s value, already reduced for an early claim or increased for a delayed one). Leave an amount blank to let the tool estimate it. To compare claim ages, save a scenario with each age and pick the two on Compare.

Defaults. A claim age of 67 with entered benefits at 62, 65, 67 and 70 for each person; the basis is Manual entry.

Logic. Estimated rows are straight lines through the entered ages: between two entered ages the line between them; below the lowest or above the highest, the line through the two nearest entered ages continued, never below zero. One entered age is used at every claim age. They are marked estimated in the basis column and are never saved, so changing an entered amount refills the rest. The model pays the benefit used at the claim age and never scales it.

On Earnings history the benefit card shows an estimate, from the earnings history entered or backtracked from a last salary (the backtracked years are read exactly like entered ones), that follows SSA’s simplified method, as its Quick Calculator does, in today’s value: each past year’s earnings are indexed to the newest year of the national average wage index and capped at this year’s wage base; today’s salary is held level from this year until pay stops or the claim age, whichever is first; the 35 largest years give the average indexed monthly earnings (AIME), this year’s bend points give the primary insurance amount (PIA, the benefit at full retirement age), and each month of claiming before full retirement age takes off 5/9 of 1% (5/12 of 1% after 36 months), each month after it adds 2/3 of 1%, up to 70. The card shows the AIME, the PIA, the full retirement age and the estimate at each claim age. Years with no earnings entered count as zero, and the card warns how many there are; with fewer than ten years with earnings no benefit is paid (a stand-in for the 40 work credits). Birth years before 1943 must use the table. The estimate mixes two wage levels (past earnings at the newest wage index year’s level, future pay at today’s salary), ignores cost-of-living adjustments and the year of eligibility, and is wage indexed but treated as today’s value, so it understates the benefit of a young earner; compare it with your Social Security statement.

Limits: each person gets only their own benefit as entered (no spousal, survivor, divorced-spouse or child benefit; a spousal top-up can be folded into the lower earner’s table amounts by hand); there is no earnings test, so a claim before pay stops is paid in full; no one dies, so a later claim is judged to the end of the plan horizon and can look better than it would for a shorter life. Taxable Social Security counts withdrawals and gains, so a later claim can change the tax on the same withdrawals; the Taxes chart shows the effect year by year.

Medicare

What it is. What replaces the health plan from the Medicare age: the Medicare type, Part B, the Part D drug plan and Medicare’s own cost sharing. The income-related surcharge (IRMAA) always applies while a Medicare premium is charged; it has no input of its own.

Defaults. Medicare starts at 65 on Original Medicare; Part B and the Part D premium are off or zero, so no IRMAA; 20% coinsurance, no out-of-pocket maximum.

Logic. The Medicare premiums are in today’s value and grow 1.5% a year above general inflation. With visit-based healthcare costs, a person at or past the Medicare age pays the visits through the Part B deductible (law data), then the Medicare coinsurance (up to the plan’s out-of-pocket maximum, if any, on Medicare Advantage; through the supplement, if any, on Original Medicare), and drugs up to the Part D yearly cap (law data); the health plan’s deductible, coinsurance, maximum and household sharing no longer apply to them. The Medicare premiums are always after tax; with IRMAA, the after-tax premiums and the healthcare costs the HSA did not pay, they are the medical expenses the deduction counts by themselves. Part B uses the law data’s monthly premium, and IRMAA looks at AGI two years earlier, so a conversion or a large withdrawal raises the premium two years later: see IRMAA and the two-year lookback. When the first two years’ IRMAA tier has to be estimated, a warning says so.

Medicare supplement

What it is. A Medigap-style supplement for each person at or past the Medicare age, on Original Medicare only: its premium, and the part of Medicare’s cost sharing it pays. It does not cover drugs.

Defaults. Off. Switched on it starts Plan G style: the deductible is the law data’s Part B deductible (medicare_part_b.annual_deductible), then 0% (the supplement pays all the rest) with no maximum, and a premium of 0 to fill in (no typical premium is shipped).

Logic. Medicare pays first. Each visit runs through the Part B deductible and the Medicare coinsurance as above; what that leaves to the person, visit by visit, is then run through the supplement’s deductible, share and maximum (grown like the visit prices), and the person pays only that. Drugs still stop at the Part D yearly cap. The premium is paid by each person at or past the Medicare age, grows like the other Medicare premiums and is part of Health Premium; it is after tax and counts toward the itemized medical expenses. What the supplement pays lowers Healthcare, and with it the itemized medical amount. Two informational summary columns, outside the outflows so nothing counts twice, show the supplement: Supplement Premium (its part of Health Premium) and Supplement Paid (the visit cost it paid, already taken out of Healthcare). After a move to another state the health premium, the supplement premium and the Part D premium follow the new state’s figures; Health Premium State Change (also informational, part of Health Premium) shows what that adds or saves (see Moving at retirement).

A scenario saved before the supplement had its own section loads with a supplement premium above 0 as a Plan G style supplement that is on with that premium, so from the Medicare age its visit costs fall while healthcare costs are included; with no premium the supplement is off. A scenario with a Medicare out-of-pocket maximum and no supplement premium loads on Medicare Advantage and keeps its results; every other scenario loads on Original Medicare, where the maximum no longer counts.

Spending in retirement

What it is. Spending that falls or rises once you retire: the rows of Expenses change by a percent from a start age.

Defaults. Off, Equal change at 0%, starting at your Social Security claim age (the age you stop working when you have no benefit entered); every row at 0%. Each percent runs from -100% (the row stops) to +100% (the row doubles).

Logic. For every year you are at or past the start age, a row’s today’s-value amount is multiplied by 1 + its percent, in one step (no ramp). Its From and To ages still apply, and inflation, the child-years factor and the row’s tax tags apply on top, so a row tagged with a deduction deducts its changed amount, a changed Charity row changes the Charitable Giving outflow (and the charity deduction and QCD with it), and an education row’s 529 withdrawal follows its changed amount. The clock is your age, also with a partner. With the switch off or every percent the choice reads at 0, the run is exactly the run without it. A scenario saved before this section loads with it off and its results unchanged. A new total typed for the walkthrough’s monthly spending question replaces the monthly rows with one, so their percents go with them.

Strategies

What it is. The three strategies that can be switched on, each in its own section, and the Relocation section. Who inherits what is left, how they take it and the terminal tax rate used to judge the strategies are not a strategy: they are the Beneficiaries section of the Benefits tab (Outputs, Summary shows the rate used with a link there). Each strategy is off by default, so a saved scenario that does not use them is unchanged; a section shows its switch, and its settings appear only while the switch is on. The rules and the law each one models are in Strategies. To compare a strategy, save one scenario with it on and one with it off, and pick the two on Compare.

Defaults. All three strategies off; a conversion window of ages 60 to 74 when switched on; one beneficiary with the whole estate, no other income, filing single, in the state you live in at the end, taking the money over 10 years; no terminal tax rate; no move to another state (Home follows me on when one is set).

Logic.

  • A conversion each year in the window moves Traditional money to a Roth account of the same owner and pays its tax now: up to the amount, and under every ceiling you set (the lowest one wins). Required distributions and the year’s withdrawals come first and are never converted. Details: Roth conversions; the IRMAA ceiling looks two years ahead, see IRMAA and the two-year lookback.

  • The backdoor deposits after-tax cash in a Traditional IRA and converts it to the Roth IRA, only from cash the year leaves and never from a withdrawal. A Traditional IRA with pre-tax money makes each conversion partly taxable (pro-rata); a warning says so when it applies. Details: Roth backdoor.

  • A QCD, from the IRA owner’s age 70.5, pays the Charity rows straight from that person’s Traditional IRAs: the distribution is not taxed and counts toward that person’s required minimum distribution. The gift is still charged in full; the part paid this way is not a deductible cash gift. Details: QCDs.

  • Strategies are compared after tax: Traditional money left at the end is taxed at each beneficiary’s brackets as their payout takes their share (over 10 years, or all at once in the year after the death), or, when you set one in Benefits, Beneficiaries, charged a flat terminal tax rate whichever the payouts. Details: Terminal tax rate.

  • Beneficiaries (Benefits tab, strategies.heirs): one card each with a name, a share of the estate (the shares total 100%), other yearly income (today’s value), filing status, income tax state (Same as household is the state you live in at the end) and payout. Each is taxed on their own share and the taxes are summed. They set how the after-tax final value is measured only, not the plan; the state you live in at the end (your home state, or the state you move to) decides the state estate and inheritance taxes.

  • Relocation (strategies.relocation, the Relocation section after the QCD): a move of both of you to another state at the start of the year you are the move age (blank: the age your pay stops, or now when retired), lived in for whole years. See Moving states below and Relocation: moving to another state.

  • The strategy warnings say what the model leaves out for the settings chosen.

  • Optimize searches these settings for the best ones.

Moving states. The state you live in each year is derived, never stored (wmti.homes.residence.residenceStates): year 0 is the home state; a primary residence bought later or a rent period starting later with a State (Home.state, RentPeriod.state) moves you to that state from its first year (a shared move year belongs to the item that starts in it); one with no state keeps the state lived in before it; a year with no primary residence and no rent period keeps the last state; a home or rent period held in year 0 never changes the home state (its state only says where the home sits, for its sale tax), and a second home never moves you. The Relocation strategy’s move is one more change in its year; the changes apply in year order, and a primary residence or rent period starting in the strategy’s move year wins. In each year lived in another state than the home state that year’s state replaces the home state, and your entered amounts, which hold the home state’s costs, change only by how that state’s figures in its law file compare with the home state’s (a ratio that state over the home state, 1 when the home state’s figure is 0), so several moves need no chaining and a year back in the home state costs as entered (statecosts.factorsByYear). The Relocation strategy’s own home or rent period (below) compares the relocation state with the state lived in the year before its move:

  • Income tax. The state income tax and the average local income tax are the new state’s.

  • Homes. The Relocation strategy moves your housing through the primary residence lane (wmti.homes.residence.relocatedLane, applied by wmti.inputs.retirementInputs.effectiveInputs to the copy the model runs; the saved homes and rent periods never change). By the lane item that covers the move year m (after the hand-over of shared years):

    • a primary home or rent period of yours starting in year m is the move itself: nothing is added; a blank state on it becomes the relocation state, and another state set on it wins (a strategy warning says the strategy’s state was ignored);

    • a primary home O: with Home follows me (homesFollow) on, O is sold at the close of year m - 1 (a later sale age moves up to m: the shared year goes to the new home), its mortgage paid off from the proceeds and its gain taxed like any sale, by the state it sits in; off, O becomes a second home (primaryResidence off: no section 121 exclusion when it is sold) and keeps its own sale. Either way a new primary home “O (S)” in state S is bought at the start of year m: its price (today’s value) is O’s expected value in year m (HomeValue.valueIn at the mean inflation, in today’s value) times the housing.typical_home_value ratio; its property tax is O’s times the property_tax.effective_rate ratio and the value ratio; its insurance O’s times the home_insurance.average_annual ratio; it keeps O’s appreciation, closing cost, transfer tax, fee, selling cost and prepayment rates, has no cost basis or sale price of its own, and is sold when O was meant to be. Financing: on, O’s mortgage carries over (same rate, extra payment and months left, the amount financed equal to O’s balance at the start of year m, the down payment floored at 0); off, a new loan with the Home defaults (20% down, 360 months, O’s rate) when O has a mortgage; no mortgage on O means all cash. Its purchase cash is in Asset Purchases, and it counts as bought in year m (a sale fewer than min_years later gets no exclusion). In inflation adjusted dollars runs its value follows the run’s inflation index from its purchase, like any home bought later;

    • a rent period O ends at year m - 1 and a rent period “O (S)” in state S starts in year m and runs to O’s end, at O’s rent times the housing cost index ratio (housing.cost_index); Home follows me makes no difference;

    • no item in year m (a gap, or no homes and no rent): nothing is added; the state alone moves.

    A move in year 0 cannot sell a home owned now the year before: the home or rent held in year 0 is re-based in place instead (the home’s value, property tax and insurance by the ratios above, its state set, its mortgage unchanged, no sale or purchase); with Home follows me off the new home is bought in year 0. Later user homes and rent periods keep their value, dates and state; a second home and every home you entered keep their entered value and costs.

  • Cars and gas. Each car’s insurance is multiplied by the car insurance ratio, its registration becomes the entered amount plus the car tax difference (car_taxes.average_annual, new less old, today’s value; at least 0) and maintenance does not change; its gallons are priced at your fuel price plus the change in the average pump price (fuel.average_price_per_gallon, new less old; at least 0), and a car bought or replaced from the move year pays its sales tax rate plus the change in the vehicle sales tax rate (sales_tax.vehicle_rate, new less old; at least 0), so a move from a state without one (Montana, New Hampshire, Oregon) adds the new state’s rate.

  • Rent. Rent is charged as entered for each rent period; the Relocation strategy’s rent period in the new state carries the housing cost index ratio (see Homes above).

  • Prices. Each Bills, Planned Spending and Other Spending row is multiplied by the ratio of its price type (ExpenseItem.priceType, expensecategories.priceRatio): grocery by price_indexes.grocery_index, goods by price_indexes.bea_goods, services by price_indexes.bea_services, none by 1, and a row with no price type (one added by hand) by the customBlend of .config/expenseCategories.json, 0.4 x goods + 0.6 x services (the goods and services shares of personal consumption outside housing and healthcare). Major Spending and Charity rows are not re-priced. The change, with the child inflation factor and the spending change applied as for the row itself, is part of State Cost Change; the category columns keep the entered amounts.

  • Sales tax. The taxable expense rows the prices above do not re-price (rows priced none and Major Spending, each taxable by its sales tax class, see below) and the random major spending (taxed at the combined rate) cost (1 + new rate) / (1 + old rate) times as much (statecosts.salesTaxRatio, model._salesTaxBases). Rows that are not taxable, Charity, the children’s college and daycare rows and the Home, Cars and Healthcare tabs take no sales tax change. The price levels are shelf prices that already hold the state’s sales tax, so a re-priced row takes no sales tax change and each dollar changes once. The price change plus this difference is the signed outflow State Cost Change, in the expenses and their total.

Sales tax classes. Each Bills, Planned Spending, Other Spending and Major Spending row has a sales tax class (expensecategories.salesTaxClass): its own ExpenseItem.taxable when set (true: general, false: none), else the default of the suggested row of the same name in .config/expenseCategories.json (taxable true, false or byState with its stateRule, grocery or clothing), else general for a row added by hand. statecosts.salesTaxRate gives each class’s rate in a state: general the sales_tax.combined_rate, grocery the sales_tax.grocery_rate (the combined rate when a file has none), clothing the combined rate unless sales_tax.clothing_taxable is false (then 0).

Sales tax for records. The entered expenses already include the sales tax of the state lived in, so the model never adds sales tax. Each year the summary column Sales Tax (records) (RECORD_COLUMNS) shows the tax inside the taxable rows paid that year and the random major spending, after any move’s change: the sum over the rows of amount x r / (1 + r), with r the year’s state’s rate for the row’s class (statecosts.embeddedSalesTax). It is in no tax, outflow, total or cash flow column; the Taxes chart draws it hatched and not counted, the taxes CSV (breakdownCsv with taxRecords) and the chart table add it as the last column, and Compare adds its median over the runs in today’s value, beside the lifetime taxes and outside every after-tax figure; each is headed Sales tax (already in expenses, shown for records).

With no move every factor is exactly 1 and every difference 0, so results are unchanged.

Headline figures

Logic. Chance of running out is the share of runs that end with a balance under $1. The figures are in the run’s dollars: inflation adjusted when the inflation adjusted switch is on, today’s value when it is off. If an input is not valid the figures are replaced by a message naming the field.

Summary and deductions

Logic. Each run draws inflation, raises, promotions, returns, random major expenses and (with visit-based healthcare) doctor visits, or the level of a flat healthcare range, afresh from the ranges you set; the seed makes the whole set repeatable, and the first run is the single run behind the headline figures. Each year the model takes the larger of the standard and the itemized deductions (separate returns must both itemize if either does). Itemized parts: mortgage interest (from Mortgage loans and rows tagged Mortgage interest), state and property tax and rows tagged State and local taxes within the SALT cap, medical costs above the floor and charity. The senior deduction and the non-itemizer charity deduction are described in the choices that change a plan. The inputs are on Expenses (rows with a deduction, Charity rows and property taxes), Loans (Mortgage loans), Benefits (premiums), Healthcare (visits and drugs or a flat amount) and Retirement (Medicare).

Charts

The Plots charts are the middle half average of the runs year by year, apart from the total balance band and the histogram.

Balances

Logic. The median and the percentile bands of the total balance are taken across runs at each age. The account balances and cash flow are the middle half average of the runs, so a stacked area adds up to that average total.

Deposits and withdrawals

Logic.

  • Each account’s RMD uses its owner’s age and birth year (the account’s owner on Accounts); an account with no owner set is yours. The joint-life table for a spouse 10 or more years younger is not modeled: the Uniform Lifetime Table is always used.

  • A person’s QCD counts toward that person’s Traditional IRA RMDs together, never toward the other person’s; a 401k’s RMD is its own. See Required minimum distributions.

  • The tax on Traditional withdrawals is the federal, state and capital gains tax with the year’s taxed Traditional withdrawals less the same tax without them, so it includes knock-ons such as more Social Security being taxed, but not the conversion tax or IRMAA. The Summary table shows the same figure as the Tax on Traditional Withdrawals column, to compare with the total taxes.

  • Amounts are in the run’s dollars.

Taxes

Logic. The kinds are the summary columns SS Tax, Medicare Tax, Federal, State and Capital Gains (LT); they come from the law data for the home state and filing status you chose. The effect of a setting such as the Social Security claim age on the tax shows up here year by year.

State rules. Each state’s law data says how it treats Social Security: exempt (none of it is taxed), federal (the part the federal return taxes) or threshold (none at or below a federal AGI limit per filing status, above it the federally taxed part up to a share of the benefits), with an optional deduction. A state’s rule that does not fit these three is approximated and flagged unconfirmed in its file and in the State rules notes. A state may also leave a share of long-term capital gains out of its income, give exemptions as non-refundable credits off the tax (never below zero) and carry an average local income tax rate (county, city and school taxes averaged over the state), counted as state tax. Its applies_to sets the base: all income charges it on the state’s taxable income, wages on the return’s wages only (pay less the pre-tax premium and the dependent care FSA, with the 401(k) deferrals, which local wage taxes count; never interest, gains or retirement income; never below zero), which is charged even in a year with no state taxable income. A file without that rate has no local tax. The State summary column holds the local tax; the Local Tax summary column is that part of it. The Taxes plot and the Taxes paid by year table show it as its own kind and State without it, so their Total is unchanged. Being part of the state tax, it counts in the itemized state and local taxes.

State figures by year. The informational summary columns State Deduction and State Exemptions (the standard deduction and the personal, dependent and senior exemptions each return gets, from the home state’s law data) and State Credits (the exemption credits the return’s bracket tax could use) are never added to a tax or total. Both tables of the Summary’s Taxes sub-tab show them with Local Tax, so the two read together.

Why law figures jump. Some tax figures are not indexed by law: only Congress or the state legislature changes them (the Social Security taxability thresholds, every state bracket and exemption, the SALT floor from 2030, the mortgage limit, the home sale exclusion, the Additional Medicare thresholds, the dependent care FSA limit, the HSA catch-up and a few more; the law data lists them). The model holds each at its last dollar amount, so it shrinks in real terms and taxes creep up, and then, after a random 3 to 7 years, restores it to its inflation-adjusted level; the steps then repeat. Congress and the state follow two separate random schedules, so federal and state taxes step in different years. Each run, the single run included, draws its own schedules from its seed, so results vary by seed like the returns do, and the same seed always gives the same steps. The first year is never affected. The statute paths stay as written: the SALT cap of 2026 to 2029, the senior deduction of 2025 to 2028 and the top IRMAA threshold (frozen through 2027, indexed from 2028). This applies to every profile; there is no setting.

Expense chart

Logic. It averages the middle half of the runs, so the child inflation factor, loan payments (unless Payment in Expenses is ticked) and healthcare costs appear where they are charged, and the random major expense shows as its average over those runs.

How Compare judges

Logic. Two scenarios run on the same random draws, so the difference comes from the settings, not luck. The comparison table judges each scenario after tax, in today’s value: the median homes and cars less loans, the median after-tax final value, the median gain over the first scenario paired run by run on the same draws, the share of runs ahead, median lifetime taxes and IRMAA, the median lifetime sales tax inside the spending (for records, outside every after-tax figure), and the break-even rate r*. A scenario that leaves less in Traditional accounts beats the first after tax when the rate on that money is above r*, and one that leaves more beats it when the rate is below r*; the last column says which side applies to each row. r* counts the estate and inheritance taxes and the homes’ selling costs too.

The after-tax value is what the beneficiaries keep. When the first scenario has a terminal tax rate, Traditional money left at the end is charged that one rate for every row; when it has none, each row taxes its own beneficiaries’ payouts at their brackets (see Terminal tax rate). Unrealized Investments gains and home gains are not taxed, since beneficiaries get a stepped-up basis; the federal and state estate taxes and the inheritance tax are charged. When two scenarios differ in their claim age, taxable Social Security counts withdrawals and gains, so a later claim can change the tax on the same withdrawals; the Taxes chart shows the effect year by year. For the claim-age limits (no spousal or survivor benefit, no earnings test, nobody dies) see Social Security. The Roth IRA part of a deposit shrinks as income passes the phase-out range in the law data; the Roth 401k has no income limit. See Terminal tax rate for how the rate is chosen.

How Optimize scores

What it is for. Finding the best settings of the strategies for your household: the Roth conversion (its ceiling and age window), the Roth backdoor, QCDs, the withdrawal order, where surplus cash goes and the Social Security claim ages (each beneficiary keeps the payout you set). It scores each setting it tries on the same random draws (the Seed on Setup, not the Runs) and changes nothing until you apply the result.

Logic. The score of a setting is a weighted average of its runs’ after-tax final worth (the middle run counts most, the tails less), plus a bonus for the share of runs that never run short or out, less a penalty on the spread between a bad-luck run (10th percentile) and a good-luck run (90th). The search moves one strategy at a time, keeps a new setting only when it is clearly better, and tries fewer runs for settings that fall behind early. Details: Finding the best settings.
