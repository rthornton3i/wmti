Using the toolkit

In short: open the app, let the walkthrough fill in the main settings, then change one thing at a time and compare. This page shows how to open it and walks through four worked examples: a retirement projection, Traditional or Roth saving, when to claim Social Security, and a loan with an extra payment. It ends with the same steps from Python.

The app and the Python API give the same results: the app only edits a RetirementInputs and shows what simulate returns (see The app).

Open the app

The app is one web page hosted on GitHub Pages: https://rthornton3i.github.io/wmti/ . It runs the Python model inside the browser, so nothing is installed and nothing you enter leaves the device. The same page fits a phone and, from 1100 px wide, a laptop or monitor (Setup, Inputs and Outputs side by side). On a phone, open it once (about 20 MB), then install it from the menu’s Install app to use it offline. The app describes every screen.

On a first visit the walkthrough (Walkthrough in The app) opens over the page and asks for the main settings one question at a time, starting from the example household; its first step offers to import a scenario file you already have; Skip for now closes it, and Walk me through it… in the menu opens it again at any time.

The page has three tabs, Setup, Inputs and Outputs: What each tab is for lists what each is for, and Model rules by group gives the defaults and logic of each group.

Every edit reruns the model. A figure marked (%) is a percent: type 3 for 3%.

A field shown in gray holds its default. Once you change it, a small circular-arrow button appears inside the field’s right edge; tap it (or reach it with Tab and press Enter) to put the default back. Its tooltip names that default.

Use case 1: a retirement projection

  1. On Setup, under Household, enter each person’s current age and pick the home state (tap the box and type a few letters of the state’s name or code to narrow the list, then tap it or press Enter). In Income, enter the salary and the age pay stops (Income). In Retirement, enter the Social Security benefit and claim age; the earnings history on Income can estimate the benefit (Social Security).

  2. In Expenses, fill in or remove the suggested monthly categories and add your own Bills, Planned and Other spending rows, the major one-off items by age, gifts to charity as Charity rows, a tax deduction on any row that is deductible (Tax deduction) (Expenses), and in Children the children’s years and costs (Children), and in Home the homes you own with their values, mortgages and property taxes and your primary residence, or the rent, and the utilities (Home).

  3. In Accounts, list every account with its kind, owner and starting value, and choose the withdrawal strategy and priority (Accounts).

  4. In Benefits, under Retirement plan, set each person’s employer match and either keep the household plan or set a yearly deposit for each 401k and IRA account (Retirement plan).

  5. Read the pinned headline figures (your current net worth, the median final worth, the average net earnings and the average yearly expense) and, on Outputs, Summary, the chance of running out. Summary and the Balances, Deposits and withdrawals and Taxes charts under Plots show the year by year middle half average of the runs behind them (see Headline figures and Outputs in The app).

Use case 2: Traditional or Roth savings

Save the open scenario under a name, change Benefits (for example, move the deferral into a Roth deposit), then Duplicate it under another name, and open Compare and pick one as Config A and the other as Config B. Both run on the same random draws, so the difference in final worth comes from the setting, not luck. How Compare judges explains the table (after-tax final value, paired gain, break-even rate) and the terminal tax rate; Compare in The app explains Settings that differ. To judge a strategy, save one scenario with it on in Strategies and one with it off, and compare the two.

Use case 3: when to claim Social Security

Enter the monthly benefit your Social Security statement shows for two or more claim ages in the benefit table on Retirement; the tool estimates the other ages from 62 to 70, or can estimate them from your earnings history on Income (Social Security explains both, the simplified SSA method and its limits). Save the scenario, change the claim age and save it under another name, then open Compare and pick the two: they run on the same random draws (How Compare judges).

Use case 4: a loan and an extra payment

In Loans, add a loan with its balance, rate in percent, months left and an extra monthly payment (a mortgage goes on its home on Home). Save the scenario without the extra payment as well and compare the two to see what it saves. When the payment is already a row on Expenses, tick Payment in Expenses so it is not charged twice (Loans).

Using the Python API

The same run from Python (run it from the repository root after pip install -e .):

import dataclasses

from wmti.retirement import compareScenarios, compareTable, loadInputs, runIterations, runOnce

inputs = loadInputs()                       # the shipped default profile
result = runOnce(inputs, seed=1)            # one seeded run
print(result.outputs["Final Account Value"])
print(result.summary[["Age (you)", "Federal", "State"]].head())

monteCarlo = runIterations(inputs, iterations=250, seed=1)
print(monteCarlo.median, monteCarlo.typical, monteCarlo.shareDepleted)

claimAt70 = dataclasses.replace(inputs, you=dataclasses.replace(inputs.you, socialSecurityAge=70))
table = compareTable(compareScenarios({"claim early": inputs, "claim at 70": claimAt70}, iterations=250, seed=1))
print(table)

Saved configs are JSON files; saveInputs and loadInputs write and read them, and a file from an older version of the toolkit is upgraded when it loads. loadInputsWithVersion also returns the file’s own schemaVersion, so a caller can tell an upgraded load. A file with no schemaVersion is read as version 0, but one that also holds homes, rents or cars (lists only newer versions save) is refused with a message asking for the version it was saved with. Whole-number fields (ages, years, months) refuse a fraction such as 40.7, and an upgrade that changes results warns, never naming the amounts. loadScenarioUpgrading (in wmti.inputs.scenarios) saves an upgraded config back at once in the current format after copying the original to .backup/<name>.v<old version>.json beside it (an existing backup is never replaced; a numbered copy is added instead). A config holds at most 50 accounts, 50 loans, 20 cars, 20 homes, 20 children, 100 salary-history years, 200 expense rows and 200 rows in any other list (LIST_LIMITS and OTHER_LIST_LIMIT in wmti.inputs.retirementInputs), and every number in it must be finite (no NaN or Infinity); a file that breaks either is refused with a message naming the field. A config’s name follows the rule described under Scenarios on Setup in The app (no __proto__ and no Windows device name such as CON).

What the toolkit does not model yet

Roth conversions, the Roth backdoor deposit and qualified charitable distributions (Strategies), the Medicare IRMAA surcharge (Retirement), the senior deduction and the non-itemizer charity deduction are in the model; Strategies says how each works. What it still leaves out (the Net Investment Income Tax, the early-withdrawal penalty and Roth five-year rules, deaths and the survivor’s single brackets, the ACA premium credit, credits and the AMT, an exact Social Security benefit calculation (the earnings estimate is simplified), spousal and survivor benefits and the earnings test, the joint-life RMD table) is listed in the Law backlog in Tax law in the model.

Inflation

The inflation range (low, most likely, high; in the Inflation card in Setup’s Advanced settings, under the inflation adjusted switch, which is off by default) is a triangular draw each year. With Inflation-adjusted dollars ticked, expenses, Social Security and the law’s indexed figures grow with each year’s drawn inflation and balances are in inflation-adjusted dollars. Unticked, the model runs in today’s value: returns and pay are deflated by the mean of the range (for 1%, 3%, 6% that is 3.33%, not the 3% most likely value), exactly as (1 + return) / (1 + inflation) - 1. The two ways therefore tell the same story; a plan that only works at a 3% inflation but not at the range’s 3.33% mean runs out of money in both.
